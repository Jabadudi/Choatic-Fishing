# Chapter 8 — Reedwater's Call

On the ninth day, I ran the perimeter eleven times without stopping.

I want to be clear about what that means. It does not mean I ran well. It means that at no point did I stop, sit down, lie down, or beg the sky for a quick and dignified death. At the end of the eleventh lap I walked through the south gate looking, I'm told, like a very sweaty ghost, and Drayfus was standing under the watchtower with his arms folded.

He looked at me for a long time.

"Your shoulders," he said, "are better."

It was the nicest thing anyone had said to me in nine days.

Then he walked to the lower rung of the tower ladder, reached behind it, and held out Grandpa's rod.

"Small casts," said Drayfus. "At the river. Not the training circle, not the village, not at any person. With a witness. And you stop the moment you feel the hollow."

"The hollow?"

"You know the one."

I did.

"Mara goes with you," he said. "If you fall in, she pulls you out. If you do something stupid, she tells me."

I took the rod. It felt different in my hands than it had the first time. Lighter, somehow. Like it had been waiting.

"Thank you," I said.

Drayfus had already turned his back.

⸻

The Willowback river was wide, brown-green and slow, with the kind of sluggish current that makes you think about nothing. It came down from the hills in the north, bent around the south side of the village, and ran out past the docks into a country of reeds and mud flats that I'd only seen from the road.

Mara picked a spot on the bank, upstream of the docks, where a flat gray rock jutted over the water like a tongue.

"Here," she said. "Nobody comes here. Nobody will see you make a fool of yourself."

"That's very kind."

"I'm being practical." She sat down on the rock with her knees pulled up and her chin on her arms. "Go on."

I stood at the edge and looked at the water.

It looked like water.

I held the rod in both hands. I did the thing Drayfus had shown me, the squat, weight in my heels, feet planted. Then I breathed in, and felt for the cool place in my chest.

> **[Still Water]** has activated.

I cast.

It was a terrible cast.

The line went about four feet, flopped into the shallows with a sound like a wet sock hitting the floor, and sat there. No blue light. No bend. Nothing.

Mara made a small noise behind her hand.

"I'm *warming up,*" I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You made a noise."

"It was a supportive noise."

I reeled in. I cast again. This time the line went six feet and tangled itself around a reed. The third time, I managed to hit my own foot.

"Are you sure," Mara said, carefully, "that you didn't just get lucky?"

I wasn't.

That was the awful part. I'd spent nine days getting my legs ready for this, and I'd done everything right: the stance, the breathing, the stillness. And the rod was just a rod, and the line was just a line, and the thing I'd done in the training circle had been somewhere between a miracle and an accident.

*Cast, and it will answer.*

"What did I do last time?" I muttered. "What was different?"

"You were scared," said the ocelot.

⸻

I didn't jump.

I've gotten better at that.

It wasn't on the rock, or the bank, or anywhere I could see. The voice came from low in my chest, quiet and close, just for me. I kept my eyes on the water and said nothing.

"Not terrified," it went on. "Not the way you were in the mud, when you thought you were going to die. But sharp. Focused. You had a target and you wanted it very badly, and you did not care how pretty it looked."

*Drayfus's sword,* I thought.

"Yes. Your mind went to one point and stayed there. The line followed." A pause. "Today, you are casting at a river. A river is not an enemy. It is not anywhere in particular. You are asking the core to bend the line toward nothing."

I frowned at the water.

*Okay,* I thought. *So I need a target.*

"You need a *purpose.* There is a difference." The voice sounded, I thought, faintly pleased. "Choose something. Anything. Do not look at the water. Look at the thing you want from it."

"Is he talking to you?" Mara asked.

I nearly dropped the rod.

"Who?"

"Nobody." She tilted her head. "You went quiet. You do that."

"I'm concentrating."

"Right," said Mara, and gave me the look again, the one with the narrowed eyes, and then went back to resting her chin on her arms. "Concentrate harder."

I looked out at the river.

I thought about what I wanted from it.

Not a fish. Not exactly. I thought about Grandpa's hands on mine, turning the reel. I thought about what he'd said, years ago, on the dock, with the rod in my little fists and my feet dangling over the planks.

*Don't look for the fish, kiddo. Look for where the fish would be.*

I'd thought it was just advice. Fisherman's wisdom. Something to say to keep a kid from fidgeting.

I looked at the river, and saw the places a fish would want to be.

The slow dark water behind the half-sunk log. The cool seam where the current folded over itself. The shadow under the overhanging willow, thick and green, where the water turned to glass.

I cast.

The line sang out, a thin bright whistle, and it didn't fly the way a line flies. It *curved.* It slid across the surface in a long smooth arc, around the log and over the seam, and settled in the shadow under the willow as softly as a leaf.

A ring of blue spread across the water from where the hook touched, slow and wide, like a pebble dropped in a bell.

> **[Reedwater's Call]**
>
> *The water answers.*

"Oh," said Mara, very quietly.

A moment later, something took the line.

⸻

It wasn't a fight. It was a conversation.

I felt it pull, a firm, patient tug, like someone leaning on a door. I leaned back. It leaned in. I gave it a little line. It took it, and then it gave it back, and somewhere in that exchange I understood that I wasn't trying to win. I was listening.

The rod hummed in my hands, a thin, living vibration, like a plucked wire, and it traveled up my arms and down into my legs and out the soles of my feet. I could feel the water through the line. I could feel its depth, its temperature, the slow weight of the current. I could feel where the fish was, a long, silver, sleepy shape in the shadow of the willow, and I could feel it deciding.

*Come on,* I thought. *Come to me.*

And it did.

It came up out of the shadow in a single smooth rush and broke the surface at my feet. A river trout, as long as my forearm, speckled gold and green, flapping on the bank with a single indignant slap of its tail.

I stared at it.

Mara stared at it.

The trout, for its part, looked unimpressed.

"You caught a fish," Mara said finally.

"I know."

"With a *fishing rod.*"

"I know!"

"I thought it was going to be… I don't know. Lightning. Fire." She had gotten to her feet without noticing. "Not a *trout.*"

I picked it up. It was cold and slick and heavy, and its heart was beating so fast I could feel it against my palm.

And I laughed.

I couldn't help it. I was standing on a rock at the edge of another world with a trout in my hands, and I'd just done the most ordinary, ridiculous, perfect thing that I could have done, and the core in my stomach was humming like a cat.

I let the fish go.

It hung in the shallows for a moment, gills pumping, and then flicked its tail and vanished.

> **Fishing** has increased.
>
> **[Reedwater's Call]** — *Cast cost reduced when the water is alive.*

"What's that mean?" I whispered.

"It means," said the ocelot, "that you have stopped hitting the river. You are asking it."

⸻

We did it again.

And again. Small casts, short and gentle, a dozen of them over the afternoon, with long rests between. I learned the shape of the hollow behind my breastbone, the way it deepened with every cast. I learned to feel it coming, a faint hush and tilt, like the moment before a sneeze, and I learned to stop before it arrived.

Some casts were easy. Some were hard. And the difference, I realized, was the water.

When I cast at the shallows by the docks, where the river was busy with small fish and drifting reeds and dragonflies skating over the surface, the cost was almost nothing. A pinch. A breath. The line went where I asked, and the river said *yes.*

When I cast into the still, dead pool behind the old pilings, where nothing moved and nothing lived, my reserves dropped like a stone.

"The land is alive," Mara said, when I told her. She was lying on the rock now, one arm behind her head, watching the clouds. "Grandma says that. She says the core isn't a power so much as a voice, and the voice only works where someone's listening."

"Who's listening?"

"The land. The water. Everything in it." She shrugged. "That's why they're called Region Cores. You're not just strong. You're *connected.*"

I thought about that.

Connected to the river. To the reeds. To the slow brown water sliding past, and the fish asleep in it, and the cold pull of the current at the end of the line.

Connected, I thought, to something I couldn't see.

"Hey, Mara."

"Mm?"

"Does your grandmother have a lot of stories about my grandfather?"

She didn't move. But her eyes closed.

"Not stories," she said. "She has… pieces. She'll mention something, and stop, and change the subject." A pause. "I used to think she was just sad."

"And now?"

"Now I think she's careful."

The silence settled over us, warm and long. The sun slid lower. The river went gold.

"Do you ever wish you'd known him?" I asked.

"I did know him," said Mara. "Sort of. He came every summer when I was small." Her voice was soft. "He gave me a carved fish once. It was a terrible carving. I think I've still got it somewhere."

I smiled.

"That sounds like him."

"He said he'd made it for his granddaughter," said Mara, "but she was too far away. So he'd give it to me, until he could deliver it himself."

My smile faded.

*Shylee.*

He'd made something for Shylee, a thing she'd never been given. She'd been a baby, and there was a carved fish in a village in another world that was supposed to be hers.

I wasn't sure what to do with that.

"I'm sorry," Mara said. "I shouldn't have—"

"No. It's okay." I gripped the rod. My voice came out rough. "It's really okay."

I stood for a moment with my back to her, looking at the water.

*I'll get you home,* I thought. *I'll get you your fish.*

The sun was nearly gone. The river had gone from gold to copper, the reeds black against it. I had one cast left. I could feel it. One small one, a pinch's worth, before the hollow opened up and swallowed me.

I wanted to end well.

I closed my eyes.

I didn't aim at anything. I didn't picture the fish, or the log, or the shadow. I just let the line go, and I thought, as clearly as I could, about the thing I wanted most in the world.

*Home.*

⸻

The rod bent double.

It happened so fast I almost lost it. One moment the line was slack, and the next it was drawn tight as a bowstring and the whole rod was bowing toward the water. My feet skidded on the stone. I dropped into the squat without thinking, heels dug in, and the weight came through my legs like a train.

"Eli!" Mara was on her feet. "What is it?"

"I don't know!"

"A fish?"

"It's *heavy!*"

It was. It wasn't pulling like a fish. A fish pulls in surges, a push and a rest, a push and a rest. This was a steady, relentless, dead weight, as if I'd hooked the bottom of the river itself.

"Let it go!" said the ocelot, urgent now, its voice sharp in my head. "Drop the line!"

"I can't!" I gritted my teeth. "It won't let me!"

The core thrummed in my stomach, hard enough to hurt. Heat flared up my arms. The words flickered in front of my eyes, flashing, unreadable. The line was glowing, blue and fierce, and the water beneath it was starting to churn.

"Mara, get back!"

"I'm *not* going to—"

I pulled.

All the weight of nine days of buckets and laps and squats went through my legs and into my back and out through my arms, and the line came up out of the water in a long, streaming arc, and something came with it.

It struck the rock with a heavy, ringing *thunk* and lay there.

For a moment, neither of us moved.

The river ran quietly on. The reeds whispered. Somewhere downstream, a frog started up, and then thought better of it.

Mara was the first to speak.

"That's not a fish."

It wasn't.

It was about the length of my hand, smooth and dark, and it was lying on the gray rock as if it had been made for it. Not rubber. Not stone. Something between the two, translucent as old glass, with a slow, sullen sheen on it like oil on water.

There were markings inside it.

Small, careful markings, suspended in the dark, like insects in amber.

They weren't glowing.

I knew that shape. I'd held that shape in my fist in a burning cabin, and in a dark room, and in my mouth.

"It's a lure," I whispered.

"Eli," said Mara, in a very careful voice. "That looks like the one you…"

"Yeah."

"But that one's *in* you."

"Yeah."

She took a step back.

I looked down at it. At the dark, still thing on the rock, with its dead, unlit markings. It looked exactly like the Region Core, if the Region Core had been switched off.

In my stomach, the real one had gone absolutely silent.

And in my head, for the first time since I'd met it, the ocelot didn't say anything at all.

I waited.

I waited a long time.

Then, very softly, in a voice I'd never heard it use, it said:

"Do not touch that."

"What is it?"

"Eli." My name, for the first time. Not *boy.* Not *fool.* "That should not be here."

"What *is* it?"

The river went on running. The sun slipped under the edge of the world. And on the rock between us, the dark thing lay perfectly still, like something that had been waiting a very long time to be found.
