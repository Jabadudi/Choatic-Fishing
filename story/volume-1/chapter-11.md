# Chapter 11 — I've Found You

There were thirty-one notches on the wall beside my bed.

I'd started carving them on the third morning, with the little knife Mara had given me for cleaning fish. One notch per sunrise. It had seemed like a good idea at the time. Something to keep track of. Something to hold on to.

Now it was mostly a way of doing math I didn't want to do.

Thirty-one days.

Thirty-one days since I'd climbed out of a dinghy onto a dock and promised my little sister a text. Thirty-one days of Shylee checking her phone. Of her calling mine, probably, and getting nothing. Of the captain telling her he'd dropped me off on the island and that was the last he'd seen of me.

I tried not to think about what thirty-one days looks like from the other side.

I mostly failed.

⸻

But I want to be fair to myself, because nobody else around here was going to do it.

A month in, I could run the perimeter eleven times without stopping, and on a good day I could do it without wanting to die. I could do forty bucket trips and still lift my arms afterward. I could hold Drayfus's squat for a full count of two hundred while he walked around me and pushed on my shoulders, and I didn't fall over.

Usually.

I could beat Mara one round in every three. She disputed this. She said it was one in four, and that the ones I won didn't count because she'd been "distracted," and when I asked by what, she hit me in the shin with the butt of her spear and said, "That."

I could cast eight times before the hollow opened up, as long as I stayed near living water. Twelve, once, at the reed beds below the docks, where the river was so thick with life that every cast felt like the water was reaching up to meet the hook. I'd caught enough trout to feed the guest lodge for a week. I'd cooked most of them. The cooking, it turned out, was the thing that made the village like me.

"You should've led with this," Mara had said, the first time, with her mouth full. "Nobody cares about the glowing rod. *This* is a skill."

And Brume watched.

Most mornings, when I came around the south corner of the path, he was there at the edge of the fields. Never closer. Never farther. Sitting with his tail around his paws in the wet grass, like a big gray stone someone had placed there on purpose. Some mornings he wasn't. On those mornings, I ran a little worse.

The ocelot said that was normal.

I asked it what "normal" meant for a man being stalked by a wolf the size of a pony.

It said, "Exactly that."

> **[Still Water]** — *Rank increased.*
>
> **[Reedwater's Call]** — *Rank increased.*

They'd appeared one morning in the middle of breakfast, glowing quietly over my porridge, and I'd stared at them so long that Mara asked if I was having a stroke.

I didn't know what *rank* meant. Nobody here could tell me. Mara said the core spoke to the chosen in its own language. Drayfus said if I wanted to know how strong I was, I could stand in the circle and find out.

The ocelot said, "Later."

So that was a month.

It was the best month I'd had in years.

That's the part I still feel strange about.

⸻

It happened on the thirty-first night.

I went to sleep the normal way. Bone-tired, full of fish, the ocelot on the stool with its eyes half closed. The window was dark. The moon was a thin sliver, nearly gone. The village was quiet except for the river and, somewhere a long way off, a single frog that had a lot of opinions.

And then I wasn't asleep anymore.

I wasn't awake, either.

It was cold. That was the first thing. Not the cold of a night breeze, or a stone floor. The cold of deep water. The kind that gets into your bones and stays there.

It was dark. Not the dark of a room with the lights off. The kind of dark that had weight. It pressed against my face and my chest and my eyes, and when I tried to breathe in, it felt like breathing through a wet cloth.

I couldn't move.

I tried. I tried to lift my hand, to sit up, to turn my head. Nothing. My body was somewhere else, a very long way away, and I was here, in the cold, in the dark, alone.

*Still Water,* I thought. *Come on. Come on.*

> **[Still Water]**

The words flickered. They were faint and blurry, like I was seeing them through ice. The cool feeling started in my chest, and then stopped, as if something had put a hand over it.

And then something in the dark breathed.

It was huge. I knew that immediately, the way you know when something big is standing behind you in an empty room. The breath was slow and deep and wet, and every time it came, the dark around me *shifted*, like the whole thing was one enormous animal, and I was somewhere inside its chest.

Then it spoke.

"*There you are.*"

The voice didn't come from anywhere. It came from everywhere. It was deep and slow and layered, like three or four voices speaking at once a half-second apart, and every syllable dragged across the inside of my skull like a nail across slate.

"*I have been looking for so long. Searching. Listening. Every night, I listened. And then.*"

The dark leaned closer.

"*You shone.*"

I couldn't breathe.

"*Like a lantern on the water. Like a fire on a hill. So bright. So very, very bright.*"

And the dark pulled back.

⸻

I was above the village.

I didn't know how. One moment I was in the cold, and the next I was in the sky, high up, looking down on Willowback from somewhere above the clouds, and the dark was all around me like a cloak.

I could see everything.

The roofs, black in the starlight. The well in the square. The training circle, with its rows of dummies. The watchtower, with its single lantern burning. The fields to the east. The river, sliding past the docks.

And the guest lodge.

It was glowing.

A soft, steady blue light, coming up through the roof like water through a crack in the ice. Pulsing. Slow. In time with a heartbeat.

*My* heartbeat.

"*There,*" said the voice, and I felt it smile. I don't know how. "*I see you. I've found you.*"

Then the world moved.

It was like a camera pulling back in a movie. The village shrank beneath me. The river lengthened. I saw the path running north and east out of Willowback, through the fields and into the trees, and the crossroads, and a little bend in the forest where three wooden chimes were swinging in a wind I couldn't feel.

The view swung east.

Past the crossroads. Past the end of the fields. Over a long, low, glittering country of reeds and black water, cut through with thin bright lines that I realized were wooden walkways, and dotted with small islands.

And in the middle of it, on the largest island, there was a shell.

An enormous, empty spiral shell, the size of a house, pale and ancient and cracked, lying in the reeds like something left behind by the tide. Moonlight on its ridges. Black water in its mouth.

I had never seen it before in my life.

The view kept moving.

Past the marsh. Past the last of the islands, where the reeds thinned out. Toward the east, toward a place where the land rose in a jagged line against the sky.

There were stone walls there. Huge ones. Old and broken and half fallen. They ran across a river valley, and in the middle of them there was a gap, a ragged wound in the stone, where water poured through in a long white roar.

And on the near side of the wall, in the ruins at its foot, there was a darkness.

At first, I thought it was a shadow. A cloud passing over the moon.

It wasn't.

It moved.

It *seethed.* It was a mass of shapes so dense they bled into each other, a tide of black, filling the ruins from wall to wall and spilling out across the valley floor. Thousands of them. Some tall, some short, some walking on two legs and some on four and some on more than four. Some of them were holding things. Axes. Spears.

All of them were turned east.

Toward me.

Then, slowly, as I watched, every single one of them turned its head.

And looked *up.*

Straight up. Straight at me.

"*Wait for me,*" said the voice, very softly, and it was almost tender. "*I'm coming. It won't be long now.*"

The darkness came rushing up toward me, faster and faster, a black wave filling my whole vision, and right before it hit, the voice said one more thing.

"*You smell of her.*"

⸻

I woke up screaming.

Not the yelling-at-the-chamber-pot kind of screaming. The real kind. The kind that tears your throat and leaves you hoarse. I was on my back, tangled in the blanket, soaked to the skin, and my heart was going so fast it hurt.

The core was burning.

Not warm. *Burning.* A hot coal in my stomach, flaring and flaring, and the light coming off it through my shirt lit up the whole room blue.

"Eli."

The ocelot was on my chest.

It had never touched me before. Not once. It was sitting on my sternum, heavy and solid and very real, and its blue eyes were inches from mine. It was looking at me, and its ears were flat back against its skull.

"Eli. Breathe."

I breathed.

> **[Still Water]** has activated.

It came all at once this time, cold and clean, like a bucket of river water poured straight down my spine. The panic dropped like a stone into a lake. My heart slowed. The core dimmed, slowly, from blinding blue to a soft, pulsing glow, and then to nothing.

I lay there, shaking.

"What," I said. My voice cracked. "What was that?"

"You know what it was."

"I don't. I don't know. I saw…" I swallowed. "There was a voice. And a marsh, and a shell, and a broken wall with a waterfall in it, and there were…"

"Shadow walkers," said the ocelot.

"Thousands of them."

"Yes."

"They *looked* at me. All of them. They looked right up at me." I was shaking harder now, despite Still Water, a fine, deep tremor that went all the way into my bones. "It said it found me. It said I *shone.* It said—"

I stopped.

"The training circle," I said slowly. "With Brume. When the core woke up, it lit up the whole circle."

The ocelot didn't say anything.

"That's what it saw. Isn't it? That's how it found me."

A long silence.

"Yes," said the ocelot. "I think so." Its tail moved once, slowly, from side to side. "I should have stopped it. I didn't think. I was…"

It didn't finish.

"You were what?"

"Glad," said the ocelot, very quietly. "I was glad to see him."

I stared at it.

It didn't look smug. It didn't look bored. For the first time since I'd met it, it looked small. It looked like what it was, a little spotted cat sitting on a man's chest in the dark, with its ears flat, and something in its eyes that looked a lot like guilt.

"It said something else," I said. "At the end. It said, 'You smell of her.'"

The ocelot went very still.

"Who's *her?*"

It looked at me for a long, long time.

"Get up," it said finally. "Get dressed. Go to the chief's house. Wake everyone. Tell them all of it." It stepped off my chest onto the blanket. "The marsh. The shell. The wall. Everything you saw."

"Even the last part?"

The ocelot paused.

"Tell them what you saw," it said. "What it means is not a thing they can help you with tonight."

⸻

I don't remember crossing the lane.

I remember pounding on the chief's door. Pounding and pounding, the way Mara had pounded on mine that first morning, until a light came on behind the shutters and the door opened and a guard I didn't know looked out at me with his spear half raised.

Then Emilia was there, in a long gray robe, her silver hair loose around her shoulders. She took one look at my face and stepped aside.

"Fetch Drayfus," she told the guard. "Now. And my granddaughter."

Out in the dark, at the edge of the fields, a wolf began to howl.

It wasn't the calling howl from the night he'd come into the circle. It was lower. Harsher. A warning. It went on and on, and before it ended, another wolf answered it from somewhere far to the north, and then another, and another, until the whole night was full of them.

Emilia closed her eyes.

"Sit," she said. "Tell me."

⸻

I told them.

The four of us sat around the low round table by the hearth, the same table where Emilia had first told me what the lure was, a month ago, a lifetime ago. Emilia had built the fire back up. Drayfus stood behind her chair with his arms folded and his sword across his back, and he hadn't taken his eyes off me since he came in. Mara sat beside me with a blanket around her shoulders and her hair in a tangle, and somewhere around the part about the voice, without saying anything, she reached under the table and took my hand.

I told them about the cold and the dark. The breathing. The voice.

I told them about the village from above, and the lodge glowing like a beacon.

When I described the marsh, the reeds and the walkways and the black water and the little islands, Mara's hand tightened.

"The shell," she said. "Tell me about the shell."

"It was huge. Big as a house. Pale. Spiral. On the biggest island, in the middle of everything."

"Shellback Glade," Mara whispered. "That's Shellback Marsh. East of the crossroads." She looked at her grandmother. "He's never been there, Grandma. He's never been east of the chimes."

"I know," said Emilia.

"Go on," said Drayfus.

I told them about the wall. The huge, broken stone wall across a river valley, with the gap in the middle where the water came roaring through.

Drayfus went very still.

"The sluice," he said. "The old sluice ruins. Beyond the marsh, at the foot of the eastern hills." His voice was flat. "Nobody's lived there in three hundred years. It's just stone and water now."

"Not anymore," I said.

And I told them what was in the ruins.

Nobody said anything for a long time when I'd finished. The fire popped. Outside, the wolves had gone quiet, but I could still feel them, the way you feel a held breath.

"How many?" said Drayfus.

"I don't know. I couldn't count. It was like… like looking at an anthill from above. They filled the whole valley."

"Shapes?"

"Every kind. Big ones, small ones. Some like the one at the cabin. Some on four legs." I swallowed. "Some on more than four."

Drayfus closed his eyes briefly.

"Infected beasts," he said. "Boar. Elk. Bear, maybe. Whatever they've taken along the way." He opened his eyes. "Were they moving?"

"No. They were waiting. Just standing there, all of them, looking east." I hesitated. "Looking at me."

"And the voice said—"

"*Wait for me. I'm coming. It won't be long now.*"

Drayfus let out a long breath through his nose.

He walked to the wall, where a big map was pinned up on boards, old and stained and marked in faded ink. I'd seen it before and never really looked at it. He laid one big finger on a small dot, and I realized it was Willowback. Then he traced east. Across the fields. Through the forest. Past a crossroads. Into a wide spread of scribbled blue that must have been the marsh. And then further, to the edge of the map, to a short, thick black line drawn across a river.

"The sluice," he said, tapping it.

"How far?" said Emilia.

"For a man on a good road, three days." He was frowning at the map. "For them, longer. They'll move only in darkness. They won't cross open water. The marsh walkways won't take their weight, not that many. They'll have to go around, the long way, south of the islands and back up along the river." He traced it with his finger as he spoke, slowly. "The moon is going dark. That will help them. They'll want the darkest nights."

"How long?" said Emilia.

Drayfus didn't answer right away. He stood with his finger on the map, and his lips moved silently, and I realized he was counting.

"Twelve nights," he said at last. "Fourteen, if the river is high."

The fire popped again.

Two weeks.

I looked down at the table. At my hand, still in Mara's, both of us holding on hard enough that it hurt.

*Two weeks,* I thought. *I can beat Mara one round in three. Maybe one in four. I can cast eight times before I fall down. And there are thousands of them.*

"Eli."

I looked up. Emilia was watching me. Her face was calm, but her hands, folded on the table, were white at the knuckles.

"Is there anything else?" she asked. "Anything at all. However small."

I hesitated.

The ocelot was sitting on the hearthstones, by the fire, where nobody but me could see it. It was looking at me. It didn't nod. It didn't shake its head. It just looked.

*Tell them what you saw.*

"At the very end," I said. "Right before I woke up. The voice said one more thing."

"What did it say?"

"It said, 'You smell of her.'"

I watched Emilia's face.

I'd learned that from Drayfus. *Watch his face.* And so I watched, and I saw it: the tiny, almost invisible flinch at the corner of her mouth. The way her folded hands tightened, just for a second. The way her eyes went to the iron-bound chest under the herb shelf, and came back, so fast I almost missed it.

"Shadow walkers say many things," she said quietly. "They are made of hunger and old hate. They lie, and they twist, and they reach for whatever will hurt." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table. "Don't carry that one tonight. Carry the rest."

I didn't believe her.

I don't think she expected me to.

But I nodded anyway, because she looked so tired, and because Mara's hand was still holding mine, and because Drayfus had already turned away from the map with the face of a man who had just been handed something very heavy and had decided to carry it.

"Wake the guard," Emilia said to him.

"They're awake," said Drayfus. "The wolves saw to that."

"Then wake the village. Quietly. At sunrise, I want everyone in the square. Every family. Every child." She stood up. In the firelight, she looked older than I'd ever seen her, and taller. "We have two weeks. We will use every hour of them."

Drayfus nodded once and went out. The door closed behind him, and I heard his voice in the lane, low and hard, giving orders.

Mara still hadn't let go of my hand.

"Eli," she said. Her voice was very small.

"Yeah?"

"You said they all looked up at you." She was staring at the fire. "In the dream. All of them."

"Yeah."

"What did that feel like?"

I thought about it.

I thought about the cold and the dark and the breathing. The tide of black filling the valley. The thousands of faces turning upward, all at once, all toward me, like flowers turning toward the sun.

"Like being seen," I said. "By something that had been looking for me for a very long time."

Mara didn't say anything.

She just squeezed my hand, once, and didn't let go.

⸻

I went back to the guest lodge before sunrise.

The ocelot was on the stool. The window was going gray. Outside, the village was waking up: doors opening, voices calling, the first clang of hammer on metal from the direction of the blacksmith's, starting early and not stopping.

I sat on the edge of the bed and looked at the wall.

Thirty-one notches.

I took out the little fish knife, and I thought about it for a long time. Then, below the first row, I started a second. I didn't carve anything. I just marked the places, lightly, with the tip of the blade. Fourteen little scratches in a line.

Fourteen spaces.

I didn't fill any of them in.

"What are you doing?" asked the ocelot.

"Counting," I said.

It looked at the wall for a while. Then it looked at me.

"It's what you did the first night here," it said. "When you couldn't sleep. You counted."

"Sheep."

"Yes."

"It worked, too." I put the knife down. "Out like a light."

The ocelot was quiet.

"I don't think this will work as well," it said.

"No," I agreed. "I don't think so either."

Outside the window, at the far edge of the fields, a long gray shape was standing in the grass. Not sitting, the way he usually sat. Standing, with his head up and his hackles raised, a silver line along his back, looking east.

Toward the marsh.

Toward the sluice.

Toward the thing that was coming.

I watched him for a long time.

Then the sun came up, and I went out to run.

⸻

**Shylee**

Thirty-one days.

I know because I wrote it on the fridge. Every morning, a new line in dry-erase marker, right under the magnet shaped like a trout that Grandpa thought was hilarious. By week three the lines had gone all the way down to the freezer door and I had to start a second column.

The police were very nice about it. That's the worst thing I can say about them. They were nice, and they wrote things down, and a man with a mustache told me that grown men go off the grid all the time, especially after a death in the family, and that my brother would probably turn up when he was ready.

My brother had not left the apartment in two months. The delivery guy thought he was dead.

He did not *go off the grid*. He doesn't know where the grid *is*.

So on day thirty-one, I took the bus to the port.

⸻

It's surprisingly hard to find a boat captain when all you have is "old guy, probably, who goes to an island, maybe."

The first four men I asked laughed at me. The fifth one asked if I was single. The sixth pointed down the dock without looking up from his phone and said, "Ask the old man. He goes places nobody else goes."

He was sitting on an upturned crate at the very end, mending a net.

I hadn't even opened my mouth when he looked up, and then he kept looking. Not the way the fifth guy had looked. The way you look at a photo you've seen a hundred times.

"You're the little one," he said.

"I'm eighteen."

"You're the little one," he said again, gently, like I hadn't argued. "Shylee. He kept your school picture in his wallet. Showed it to me every single trip. You had a gap in your teeth."

"I was seven."

"He didn't care." His face did something complicated. "I'm sorry about your grandpa, sweetheart. I told your brother the same."

My heart did a stupid little jump.

"You saw Eli?"

"Month ago, give or take. Took him out to Oscar's island. Sick over the side the whole way, same as when he was a boy." He smiled, and then the smile went away. "I wrote my number on his hand. He never called. I figured he was staying a while. Figured he needed it."

"He didn't call *anyone*."

The old man put the net down.

He looked at me for a long time. Then he looked out at the water, toward somewhere I couldn't see, the way Grandpa used to look out of the window on the nights he didn't think anyone was watching.

"Get in the boat," he said.

⸻

I didn't throw up.

I want that on the record. Eli threw up the whole way out, twice, and I didn't throw up once, and if he's alive I'm going to tell him about it every Thanksgiving until one of us dies.

If he's alive.

I stopped thinking about that and watched the island come up out of the haze instead. Trees. A little dock. A path going up through tall grass.

And at the top of the path, where the cabin should have been, something black.

"Oh," said the old man quietly. "Oh, no."

⸻

It used to be a cabin. You could still tell, if you squinted. Four walls, mostly. The doorframe. A chimney standing up out of the middle of it like a gravestone.

The roof was gone. Something had split it straight down the middle and peeled it open, and everything underneath was charred to the color of coal. The grass around it was burned in long black streaks, the way grass burns when it's lightning and not a campfire.

"Storm," the captain said behind me. He'd stopped at the bottom of the path, one hand on the dock post, like he didn't want to come any closer. "There was a bad one, the night I dropped him. Worst in years." He swallowed. "Shylee. Honey. Maybe we should call somebody."

"In a minute."

"I'll just— I'll be right here. With the boat. You holler."

"In a minute," I said again, and walked up the path.

⸻

There was no body.

I need you to understand that I checked. I went through every room, which took about eleven seconds, because there were only two of them. I looked under the bed frame. I looked in the fireplace. I went through the ashes with a stick like a crazy person, and all I found was a melted tin cup and a shelf on the far wall that had somehow not burned at all.

It was empty. There was a clean spot in the dust in the middle of it, about the size of my hand. Like something had sat there for a very long time, and then somebody had picked it up.

I stood there with my stick and I pressed my lips together, hard, the way I used to.

Nobody was there to see me do it. I did it anyway.

Then I went back outside, because I couldn't breathe in there, and that's when I saw the circle.

It was just past the cabin, in the clearing. A ring of grass, maybe ten feet across, that wasn't burned like the rest. It was *dead*. Gray. Every blade lying flat and pointing out from the center, like something had stood in the middle of it and pushed.

And it was cold.

I don't mean chilly. I mean I stood at the edge of it, in the sun, in the middle of the afternoon, and my breath came out white.

"Eli?" I said. I don't know why. It was a stupid thing to say to a patch of grass.

The grass didn't answer.

Something in the trees did.

⸻

It wasn't a sound, exactly. It was more like the sound stopped. The birds. The wind. The water slapping the dock. All of it went quiet at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone walks in that everyone is afraid of.

At the edge of the clearing, between two trees, there was a man.

He was very tall, and he wasn't moving, and I couldn't see his face. Not because it was dark. It was three in the afternoon. I couldn't see it because there was nothing there to see. Just shadow, sitting where a face should be, the way water sits in a hole.

"Hi," I said. My voice came out tiny. "I'm looking for my brother."

The shadow tilted its head.

When it spoke, it wasn't one voice. It was a lot of them, layered on top of each other, like a choir that had forgotten the words.

*"There's the other one."*

I ran.

I'd like to tell you I ran toward the boat. I didn't. I ran toward the dock *path*, which was behind him, so really I ran in a big panicked circle, and I got about six steps before the cold caught me.

It went in through my feet. Up my legs. Into my chest, and it squeezed, and the world went gray around the edges like the grass in the circle.

The last thing I heard was the old man, very far away, shouting my name.

The last thing I saw was the trout-shaped magnet on the fridge at home, which I wasn't looking at, which was miles away, which I don't know why I saw.

Thirty-one lines.

Nobody was going to write the thirty-second.
