# Chapter 4 — Region Core

"We're here," she said.

We'd stopped in front of the biggest house in the village. It wasn't a palace or anything. Still just timber and stone like the rest. But the timber had been painted, deep red with the trim picked out in white, and there were carvings around the door frame, and a little covered porch, and generally it had the vibe of a building that belonged to somebody in charge.

The two guards by the door confirmed it.

They were big. They had spears. One of them had a scar running through his eyebrow, which made it look like he was permanently unimpressed with me, which, to be fair, was probably accurate.

They looked at me.

They looked at her.

They stepped aside.

Inside, it was warm and smelled like woodsmoke and something herbal. An old woman stood at the hearth with her back to us, pouring steaming water from a kettle into a clay pot. Tea, from the look of it. A man was standing beside her, talking in a low voice. Not a farmer. He had the build and the scars and the very big sword of someone who fought for a living.

The old woman turned, saw us, and her eyes went to me and stayed there.

She said a quiet word to the man. He gave me a long, measuring glance on his way out, the kind that suggested he was working out how long I'd last in a fight, and that the number was short.

The door closed behind him.

"Sit, sit," the old woman said, waving us toward a low round table near the fire. It was one of those tables that's too small to be a dining table and too big to be a coffee table, and I sat down at it with the general caution of someone who'd been nearly murdered recently.

The girl sat next to me. Then she paused, looked at me, and seemed to realize something for the first time.

"This is my grandmother," she said. "She's chief of the village. And—" She stopped. Winced. "Ah. We never actually introduced ourselves, did we?"

We had not.

I'd been rescued, fed half a burned fish, and marched through a monster village by this girl, and I didn't know her name.

"I'm Mara," she said.

"Eli."

Mara turned to her grandmother, and the smile that broke across her face was the first one I'd seen from her that wasn't sarcastic.

"Grandma," she said, "this is Oscar's grandson."

The old woman went very still.

"Is this true?"

She was looking right at me. Her face was lined and brown from years of sun, and her hair was long and silver and tied back, and there was something in her expression that I couldn't quite name. Hope, maybe. The careful kind. The kind that's been disappointed before.

"Yeah," I said, a bit lost. "Oscar was my grandpa."

She put down the kettle.

She came around the table and took my face in both her hands, gently, like she was checking it was real, the way I'd pinched my own hand outside.

"Oh, my," she said softly. "Look how you've grown. I can't believe it's really you."

*Look how you've grown?*

I'd never seen this woman in my life.

Had I?

Before I could work out how to ask, her face changed. It folded in on itself, just slightly, around the eyes.

"If you are here," she said, "then Oscar is gone."

It wasn't a question.

"He passed away a few months ago," I said. "But how did you know that? How do you know my grandpa at all?"

She let go of my face and sat down across from me, slowly, the way old people sit when their knees have opinions.

"Oscar never stopped talking about you," she said. "Never. He loved you so much, Eli. He was so proud to have you as his grandson. You were his treasure. More than his own son, I think, and he'd have been embarrassed to hear me say it."

I'd been doing pretty well, up to that point.

I had been struck by lightning, attacked by a faceless axe murderer, rescued by a wolf the size of a pony, and introduced to a dog with frost scales. I had handled all of it. Badly, but I had handled it.

That sentence, I did not handle.

My eyes went hot all at once. I tried very hard not to cry in front of two women I'd just met. I lost. Quietly, but I lost.

I missed him so much.

I'd spent years letting him do all the talking. Years assuming there'd be time later. And it turned out he had been out here telling a woman I'd never met how proud he was of me, when I'd never done one thing worth being proud of.

I wished I'd been better for him.

"What's wrong?" Mara asked softly, leaning in.

Her grandmother let out a small giggle. Not a mean one. The kind you'd give a kid who'd just fallen over and was deciding whether or not to cry about it.

I wiped my face fast with the heel of my hand and took a breath.

"I'm fine," I said. "Just miss my grandpa. That's all."

Neither of them said anything to that. They didn't need to.

I took another breath.

"I want to go home," I said.

The old woman poured the tea before she answered. Three cups. She slid one across to me, and I wrapped my hands around it mostly for something to do.

"I'm not sure I know how to help you with that," she said. "But I know what Oscar told me. He used special stones, found on the slopes of the Ember Volcano, far to the northeast, beyond the Crystal Lakes. He used them to gather enough mana to jump through space." She shook her head slightly. "I never fully understood what he meant by that. Not even today. But I knew he came from another world. He was truly a powerful and extraordinary man."

I didn't drink the tea.

I didn't do anything.

*Mana.*

*Jump through space.*

*Another world.*

It clicked.

It didn't so much click as fall on me, like a piano dropped from a window. Not an island. Not some weird village with bad zoning laws and an animal-breeding problem. A completely different *world.*

Grandpa's stories.

The stone road under the lake. The wolves that walked the shore at night and kept the fishermen safe. The girl who whistled and made the fish jump into the basket. He told them a hundred times and the details never changed. Not once. Even when I tried to catch him out.

Because he wasn't making them up.

He was *remembering* them.

I'd thought they were bedtime stories. Things he invented to keep a kid entertained on a boat. And the whole time, he was telling me exactly where he'd been.

*Holy shit.*

This was insane.

"Is… everything okay?" Mara asked slowly, in the voice you use on someone who might be about to faint.

I realized I had no idea what my face was doing. All of it at once, probably. The excitement, the shock, the dawning sense of exactly how stupid I'd been for twenty years, and underneath all of it, a cold, quiet terror that was getting less quiet by the second.

"Yeah," I said. "Sorry. All good."

I was not all good.

But I knew one thing for certain. I had to get home. Somehow.

"I need those stones," I said, and stood up, fast, like I was going to march straight out the door and up a volcano right then. "I need to get back home—"

Heat flared against my thigh, and a jolt shot out of my pocket and down my leg.

"*Ow!*"

Both of them jumped to their feet. Mara's grandmother nearly knocked over the teapot, and both of them reached for me at the same time.

"Are you okay?" they said, in perfect sync.

"Yeah. Yeah, I'm fine." I hissed through my teeth and pressed my hand to my thigh. "It's just this stupid lure. I grabbed it from Grandpa's cabin."

So much for keeping it hidden.

I dug it out of my pocket. It was still hot enough to sting my fingers. It lay across my palm, soft and heavy, dark and translucent like old blue amber, worn and scratched and ancient, the strange markings suspended inside it glowing faintly blue.

Pulsing.

Bright.

Dim.

Bright.

"It's been glowing in time with my heartbeat," I said, "ever since I found it."

Neither of them answered.

Mara stared at the lure. Then at me. Her grandmother stared at the lure. Then at me. And the look on both of their faces was the exact same look, and it was not the look you give a guy who has just been zapped by his own fishing tackle.

It was awe.

And something that looked a lot like hope.

"You are truly his grandson," the old woman said quietly.

She didn't touch it. She looked like she wanted to, and decided she shouldn't.

"It's called a Region Core," she said. "The one you're holding belongs to Reedwater. Only those the land itself chooses can resonate with a core, and take its power."

I looked down at the thing in my hand.

At the blue light pulsing in time with my heart.

*Chosen by the land.*

This world was insane. Truly, completely surreal.

"To accept its power," she went on, "you have to ingest it."

I looked up.

"I have to *what*?"

"Ingest it."

"Like… eat it?"

"Yes."

I looked at the lure. It was the size of my finger, and it glowed. People don't eat things that glow.

"I'll think about it," I said.

I put it very carefully back in my pocket.

"How do I get to this Ember Volcano?"

The old woman sat back down, slowly, and folded her hands on the table.

"North of the village, at the edge of the Whispering Forest, there is a great hollow tree. We call it the Listening Tree. The elk spirit lives there." She said *elk spirit* the same way Mara had said *shadow walker*, like it was a perfectly ordinary pair of words. "If it chooses to, it can take you close enough to the volcano."

"If it chooses to," I repeated.

"It's rough country between here and there. Rougher the further north you go." She looked at me steadily. "Your journey will not be easy. You'll need proper gear, and you'll need to be much stronger than you are now."

That was polite of her.

I was aware that *much stronger than you are now* was a generous way of describing a guy who'd been knocked out by his own fishing lure.

"Stay here two full moons," she said. "I'll teach you what this world has to offer. I will prepare you for your journey."

Two months.

Two months of something. I didn't know what. Training. Monsters. Possibly eating fishing tackle.

But it was a plan. It was the first plan I'd had since I woke up in the mud.

"Okay," I said. "Thank you. Seriously. Thank you so much, uh…"

I trailed off and looked at her hopefully.

She smiled. For a second, a much younger woman looked out of that face. Long dark hair. Laughing at something just off to one side.

I'd seen that laugh before. Somewhere.

"Emilia," she said. "Just Emilia."

Then she turned to her granddaughter, and the chief came back into her voice.

"Mara. Find the boy a place to rest in the guest lodge. Both of you come back here tomorrow at sunrise, and I'll have Drayfus begin your training."

Mara lit up like someone had just handed her a birthday present.

"Thank you, Grandma!"
