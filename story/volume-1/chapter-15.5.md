# Chapter 15.5 — The Other One

*Shylee*

Thirty-one days.

I know because I wrote it on the fridge. Every morning, a new line in dry-erase marker, right under the magnet shaped like a trout that Grandpa thought was hilarious. By week three the lines had gone all the way down to the freezer door and I had to start a second column.

The police were very nice about it. That's the worst thing I can say about them. They were nice, and they wrote things down, and a man with a mustache told me that grown men go off the grid all the time, especially after a death in the family, and that my brother would probably turn up when he was ready.

My brother had not left the apartment in two months. The delivery guy thought he was dead.

He did not *go off the grid*. He doesn't know where the grid *is*.

So on day thirty-one, I took the bus to the port.

⸻

It's surprisingly hard to find a boat captain when all you have is "old guy, probably, who goes to an island, maybe."

The first four men I asked laughed at me. The fifth one asked if I was single. The sixth pointed down the dock without looking up from his phone and said, "Ask the old man. He goes places nobody else goes."

He was sitting on an upturned crate at the very end, mending a net.

I hadn't even opened my mouth when he looked up, and then he kept looking. Not the way the fifth guy had looked. The way you look at a photo you've seen a hundred times.

"You're the little one," he said.

"I'm eighteen."

"You're the little one," he said again, gently, like I hadn't argued. "Shylee. He kept your school picture in his wallet. Showed it to me every single trip. You had a gap in your teeth."

"I was seven."

"He didn't care." His face did something complicated. "I'm sorry about your grandpa, sweetheart. I told your brother the same."

My heart did a stupid little jump.

"You saw Eli?"

"Month ago, give or take. Took him out to Oscar's island. Sick over the side the whole way, same as when he was a boy." He smiled, and then the smile went away. "I wrote my number on his hand. He never called. I figured he was staying a while. Figured he needed it."

"He didn't call *anyone*."

The old man put the net down.

He looked at me for a long time. Then he looked out at the water, toward somewhere I couldn't see, the way Grandpa used to look out of the window on the nights he didn't think anyone was watching.

"Get in the boat," he said.

⸻

I didn't throw up.

I want that on the record. Eli threw up the whole way out, twice, and I didn't throw up once, and if he's alive I'm going to tell him about it every Thanksgiving until one of us dies.

If he's alive.

I stopped thinking about that and watched the island come up out of the haze instead. Trees. A little dock. A path going up through tall grass.

And at the top of the path, where the cabin should have been, something black.

"Oh," said the old man quietly. "Oh, no."

⸻

It used to be a cabin. You could still tell, if you squinted. Four walls, mostly. The doorframe. A chimney standing up out of the middle of it like a gravestone.

The roof was gone. Something had split it straight down the middle and peeled it open, and everything underneath was charred to the color of coal. The grass around it was burned in long black streaks, the way grass burns when it's lightning and not a campfire.

"Storm," the captain said behind me. He'd stopped at the bottom of the path, one hand on the dock post, like he didn't want to come any closer. "There was a bad one, the night I dropped him. Worst in years." He swallowed. "Shylee. Honey. Maybe we should call somebody."

"In a minute."

"I'll just— I'll be right here. With the boat. You holler."

"In a minute," I said again, and walked up the path.

⸻

There was no body.

I need you to understand that I checked. I went through every room, which took about eleven seconds, because there were only two of them. I looked under the bed frame. I looked in the fireplace. I went through the ashes with a stick like a crazy person, and all I found was a melted tin cup and a shelf on the far wall that had somehow not burned at all.

It was empty. There was a clean spot in the dust in the middle of it, about the size of my hand. Like something had sat there for a very long time, and then somebody had picked it up.

I stood there with my stick and I pressed my lips together, hard, the way I used to.

Nobody was there to see me do it. I did it anyway.

Then I went back outside, because I couldn't breathe in there, and that's when I saw the circle.

It was just past the cabin, in the clearing. A ring of grass, maybe ten feet across, that wasn't burned like the rest. It was *dead*. Gray. Every blade lying flat and pointing out from the center, like something had stood in the middle of it and pushed.

And it was cold.

I don't mean chilly. I mean I stood at the edge of it, in the sun, in the middle of the afternoon, and my breath came out white.

"Eli?" I said. I don't know why. It was a stupid thing to say to a patch of grass.

The grass didn't answer.

Something in the trees did.

⸻

It wasn't a sound, exactly. It was more like the sound stopped. The birds. The wind. The water slapping the dock. All of it went quiet at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone walks in that everyone is afraid of.

At the edge of the clearing, between two trees, there was a man.

He was very tall, and he wasn't moving, and I couldn't see his face. Not because it was dark. It was three in the afternoon. I couldn't see it because there was nothing there to see. Just shadow, sitting where a face should be, the way water sits in a hole.

"Hi," I said. My voice came out tiny. "I'm looking for my brother."

The shadow tilted its head.

When it spoke, it wasn't one voice. It was a lot of them, layered on top of each other, like a choir that had forgotten the words.

*"There's the other one."*

I ran.

I'd like to tell you I ran toward the boat. I didn't. I ran toward the dock *path*, which was behind him, so really I ran in a big panicked circle, and I got about six steps before the cold caught me.

It went in through my feet. Up my legs. Into my chest, and it squeezed, and the world went gray around the edges like the grass in the circle.

The last thing I heard was the old man, very far away, shouting my name.

The last thing I saw was the trout-shaped magnet on the fridge at home, which I wasn't looking at, which was miles away, which I don't know why I saw.

Thirty-one lines.

Nobody was going to write the thirty-second.
