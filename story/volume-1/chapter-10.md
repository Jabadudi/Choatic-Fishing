# Chapter 10 — The Moonfang

On the seventeenth night, the howling woke me.

It wasn't close. It came from somewhere beyond the fields, long and low and rising, the way a wolf calls when it wants one particular person to hear. I lay on my back in the dark and listened to it climb, and break, and fall away. Then it started again.

I knew that voice.

I hadn't heard it since the night of the storm. But I'd been lying in the mud with lightning in my pocket, watching a man with an axe walk toward me one slow step at a time, and just before the end, there had been a howl exactly like that one.

I sat up.

"You hear it too," I said.

The ocelot was on the stool. It had been there when I fell asleep, tail around its paws, eyes shut. They were open now. They had gone very bright, and very still.

"Yes."

"That's the one. From the storm."

"Yes."

"Why does it sound like it's calling me?"

The ocelot didn't answer. It looked at the door for a long moment, in a way I'd never seen it look at anything. Not smug. Not bored. Something a lot closer to wary.

"Go," it said at last. "Quietly. Do not take the rod."

"You're not coming?"

"I will be there," said the ocelot, "in the way that I am."

I didn't like that answer. I put on my boots anyway.

⸻

The village was asleep.

I had never seen it like this. The lanes were silver and empty. Every window was dark except one, high up in the watchtower, where a single lantern burned. The moon was nearly full. It hung over the roofs like a coin someone had forgotten to pick up, and it made every shadow sharp enough to cut yourself on.

I walked past the chief's house. Past the well. Past the training circle, where the straw dummies stood in their rows like tired sentries, and the fence cast long dark bars across the dirt.

And there, on the far side of the circle, sitting in the middle of the moonlight with its back straight and its tail curled neatly around its paws, was the wolf.

I stopped.

I had forgotten how big it was.

You think you remember a thing like that. You tell yourself that it was dark, and you were terrified, and the lightning made everything look bigger. But it hadn't been the lightning. The Moonfang was *enormous.* Its head, held high, was level with my hip. Its shoulders were as broad as a pony's, its chest deep and heavy, and its fur was the color of old silver, the kind that's been handled for so many years that it has gone soft at the edges.

It was looking straight at me.

Its eyes were pale. Not yellow, not amber. A cold, clear gray, like water under ice.

I didn't move.

I'd like to say that was bravery, but it was mostly that my legs had stopped answering to me. Somewhere in the back of my head, Drayfus's voice said, *Your power is in your legs,* and I took a very slow breath, and I let my weight settle into my heels.

> **[Still Water]** has activated.

The cool feeling slid through my chest. It didn't take the fear away. It just put it somewhere I could look at it.

*Okay,* I thought. *He's not attacking. He's sitting. He saved my life. He's sitting there like a very big, very patient dog.*

"Hi," I said.

My voice came out about half the size I'd intended.

The wolf tilted its head.

"I'm, uh. I'm Eli. I don't know if you remember. You, um." I swallowed. "You pushed a guy into the mud. For me. I never said thank you."

The wolf got up.

⸻

It moved without a sound. That was the strangest part. Something that size should have shaken the ground, and instead it crossed the training circle like a shadow that had decided to be solid. It stopped about six feet away from me. Its breath came out in two slow clouds that hung in the cold air and drifted apart.

Then it looked at my stomach.

Not my face. Not my hands. My stomach, right where the core had settled, like it could see through the shirt and the skin to the faint warm thing glowing underneath.

I felt the core stir.

It didn't thrum, or pulse. It was a different feeling, a slow, deep, answering ripple, like a stone dropped into a very still lake a very long way off. It rose up through my chest, and up my throat, and the back of my eyes went hot.

*What is this?*

"He knows," said the ocelot.

It was very quiet. It was closer than before, somewhere just behind my ribs.

"Knows what?"

"What you are carrying." A pause. "And who carried it before."

The wolf's eyes moved.

It was a tiny movement. Barely a flicker. Its pale gaze slid off my stomach and came to rest on the empty air a little to my left, at about the height of a house cat's head, where there was nothing at all.

I held my breath.

The wolf looked at the nothing.

And then it did something I had never seen an animal do. It lowered its head. Slowly, deliberately, the way you'd bow to someone you hadn't expected to see again.

*It can see you,* I thought.

The ocelot didn't answer.

*It can see you, can't it? Why can it see you? Mara can't. Emilia can't. Drayfus—*

"Do not," said the ocelot, in a voice so thin and strange that I stopped. "Not now. Please."

Please.

I had never heard it say that word.

I looked down at the wolf, and the wolf looked back at me. And I understood that whatever was going on between the two of them, a thing that had happened before I was born and wasn't going to be explained tonight, I wasn't the one it was for.

"Okay," I whispered. "Okay."

The wolf took a step forward.

⸻

I let it come.

It crossed the last six feet in three long strides and stopped with its muzzle a hand's width from my chest. I could feel the heat coming off it. It smelled like pine and rain and old smoke. It breathed in, slow and deep, along the length of my shirt, and I stayed absolutely still.

Then it pressed its nose against my stomach.

It was cold and damp and gentle, and the moment it touched me, the core woke.

I don't know how else to describe it. It *woke.* Blue light burst out through my shirt in a long, rolling pulse, and the whole training circle went bright. Every fence post, every dummy, every blade of grass stood out in sharp, shining blue. The wolf didn't flinch. It closed its eyes.

And I wasn't in the circle anymore.

⸻

It was daytime.

The light was warm and gold, and the air smelled like salt and cut reeds. I was standing, or something like standing, at the end of a wooden dock that I knew very well. I'd walked out onto it two weeks before, carrying a backpack, trying to get one bar of signal.

The cabin was behind me, whole and clean, with smoke rising from the chimney.

And at the far end of the dock, sitting with his legs hanging over the water, was an old man.

He had a gray beard, a worn fishing jacket and a rod across his knees. He didn't look like the grandfather I remembered. Not exactly. His hands were thinner. His shoulders were bowed. There was something about the set of his back, a heaviness, like a man who had been carrying a very large thing for a very long time and was only now putting it down.

He was stroking the neck of the wolf beside him.

It was the same wolf. Younger, maybe. Its fur was darker. But the same long pale eyes, the same broad chest, the same patient stillness. It had its head on his knee, and his hand moved slowly through its silver fur, over and over, as if he'd forgotten how to stop.

"You waited," Grandpa said.

His voice was exactly as I remembered it, soft and rough at the edges, like a river stone.

"All this time. Everyone told me you'd have moved on." He gave a short laugh. It didn't last. "I'm sorry I took so long, old friend. I'm so sorry."

The wolf made a low sound in its chest.

"I don't think I'm going to be able to come back much longer. My knees. My heart." His hand stopped. "But I left something for him. The boy. I left it where he'd find it. I don't know if it's right, I don't know if I've done right by him, but it's all I had left."

He bent his head. His shoulders shook.

"When he comes," said Grandpa, very softly, "be there. Please. He won't know what he's doing. He never does. He'll be loud and scared and he'll talk too much and he'll probably get seasick."

He laughed again, wetly.

"But he'll try. He always tries. Be there for him."

The wolf lifted its head. It looked at him, and then it licked the tears off his weathered cheek, once, slowly, as if it were putting a seal on a promise.

I was crying.

I didn't know when it had started. I was standing on that dock, in a vision, in a body that wasn't quite mine, with my hands over my mouth, and I couldn't stop.

*Grandpa.*

He couldn't hear me.

He turned his face toward the water, and the light shifted, and everything went white.

⸻

I came back on my knees.

I was in the dirt in the middle of the training circle, and the blue light was fading, and my face was wet. The wolf was standing over me. It had lowered its great silver head until its forehead rested against mine, and it was perfectly still.

Its breath was warm on my face.

"He asked you," I managed. "He asked you to be there."

The wolf didn't answer. It was a wolf.

But it leaned its whole weight into me, solid and warm, and stayed there. And I put my arms around its neck, because I didn't know what else to do, and buried my face in fur that smelled like pine and rain and the very faint ghost of a fishing jacket.

I didn't say anything for a long time.

Neither did the ocelot.

⸻

"Moonfang do not come inside the fence."

Drayfus's voice came from the dark edge of the circle, low and level.

I looked up.

He was standing at the gate in his cloak, bareheaded, with one hand resting on the hilt of the great sword across his back. I don't know how long he'd been there. The lantern in the watchtower had gone out. Behind him, two guards stood frozen in the lane with their spears half raised, staring at the wolf like it was a very large, very beautiful problem.

"Not in thirty years," said Drayfus. "I have been guard captain for twenty. When shadow walkers come, they fight beside us at the edge of the fields. The rest of the time they stand and watch, and they go." His eyes didn't leave the wolf. "They don't come in."

The wolf lifted its head and looked at him.

It was a very long look. Neither of them blinked.

Then the wolf, slowly and deliberately, lowered itself onto the dirt beside me, laid its chin between its front paws and closed its eyes. It looked, from a certain angle, like a gigantic gray dog that had just been told it could stay.

Drayfus let out a breath through his nose.

He took his hand off the sword.

"Put the spears down," he told the guards, without turning. "Go back to your posts. Say nothing of this to anyone until the chief has heard it."

They went.

He didn't. He came into the circle and stopped a few paces from me, looking down at the wolf, and I saw his jaw working, the scar pale along the line of it.

"You've been crying," he said.

"Yeah."

"Why?"

"I saw my grandfather."

Drayfus was quiet. The moonlight lay along the edge of his face, and for a moment, I thought he was going to say something else. Something about Oscar, or my grandmother, or any of the hundred things he hadn't told me.

Instead, he said, "Does it hurt?"

I thought about it.

"No," I said. "I don't think so. It just feels like someone put something back."

He nodded slowly.

"Go to bed," he said. "Tomorrow, you run double."

"Drayfus—"

"*Double,*" he said, and walked away, and I noticed that he didn't look back, and that his hand, as he left, was shaking just slightly.

⸻

"His name is Brume," said Emilia.

It was morning, and the kitchen was warm, and Mara was staring at me over a bowl of porridge with her mouth very slightly open. News had spread through the village before the sun was fully up. A *Moonfang inside the fence.* A *Moonfang asleep in the training circle, with its head on the boy's foot.* Half the village had come to look. The other half had pretended they hadn't.

The wolf was gone. He had slipped out of the village at first light, and all that was left in the training circle was a ring of flattened grass where he had lain. I could see it through the window, and a small boy crouched beside it, solemnly measuring it with his arms.

"He has guarded the Reedwater shore for longer than I have been alive," Emilia went on. She was sitting across from me with a cup of tea she wasn't drinking. "Longer than my mother. The Moonfang do not take names from us. We gave him ours, and he tolerated it."

"He knew my grandfather," I said.

"He loved your grandfather." She said it simply. "When Oscar first came to this world, Brume was the one who found him. A very small, very frightened man on a very large beach, who didn't have a word of our language and was trying to catch a fish with his bare hands." She smiled, a little, into her cup. "Brume sat on the sand and watched him fail for an entire afternoon. Then he went into the water and drove a fish to shore. I believe it was a joke."

Mara made a small, startled sound, half laugh, half hiccup.

"After that, they were inseparable," said Emilia. "Oscar went out with the Moonfang on their night rounds. They taught him the shoreline. He taught them whatever it is that a man from another world has to teach a wolf. I never asked." Her smile faded. "And then, one summer, he stopped coming."

"His knees," I said. "He stopped going out to the island when his knees gave out."

"Yes." She turned the cup. "He stopped coming. And Brume waited."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"The Moonfang don't choose a person," Emilia said. "They choose to wait. It is the only thing they do that we have never understood. A wolf will watch a door for fifty winters if it believes someone is coming through it." She looked at me. "He went to the cabin every night. Every single one. He sat on the shore and watched the water, and he did not stop, even when everyone told him that Oscar was too old to come back."

"That's why he came for me," I said slowly. "The night of the storm."

"It was a shadow walker I was chasing," Mara said. Her voice was very small. "It broke away from the fight and went straight for the cabin, and I couldn't catch it. I was too slow. By the time I got there, you were on the ground, and it was almost on top of you." She swallowed. "And then something came out of the dark and hit it. I didn't know it was Brume."

I stared out the window at the ring of flattened grass in the morning light, and thought about a man on a dock, years ago, waiting for somebody who was late.

And a wolf on a shore, waiting for the same man.

*Maybe they'd both been waiting for each other,* I thought. *Neither of them knew the other one had stopped.*

I looked at the ocelot, in the corner, which no one else could see.

It was looking out the window at the empty circle. It didn't say anything. But I thought its tail, curled neatly around its paws, was trembling very slightly.

"Eli," said Emilia.

I turned.

"The Moonfang choose once," she said. "And they choose slowly. He came to look at you. Whether he chooses you is for him to decide, and I don't think he will decide it quickly." Her eyes were bright and wet and very kind. "If he does, it will be a great gift. And a great weight."

"I'll wait," I said, and meant it.

She nodded. She looked like she wanted to say something else. She didn't.

Instead, she reached across the table and put her hand over mine, briefly, just for a moment, and squeezed.

⸻

I ran double that day.

Brume watched.

He was sitting at the far edge of the fields, a long silver shape against the green, ears forward, perfectly still. Every time I came around the south corner of the path, he was there. He never moved closer. He never looked away. The guards along the perimeter watched me go by in total silence, and then one of them, an older man with a gray mustache, looked out at the wolf and put a fist over his heart.

I didn't know what that meant. I didn't have time to ask.

On the eleventh lap, Mara fell in on my right.

"You," she said, without looking at me, "are going to be insufferable about this."

"I'm not."

"You are. You've got a *Moonfang* watching you."

"He's not mine. He's, uh. He's not a pet. He hasn't chosen anything."

"He came *inside the fence* for you, Eli. In thirty years, no one has had that." She was grinning, out of breath, her eyes very bright. "I can't *believe* I have to train with you."

I laughed.

I couldn't help it. It came up from somewhere deep, a big, helpless, breathless laugh, and I was still laughing when I crossed the finish line at the foot of the watchtower and went down face-first in the grass.

Out at the edge of the field, Brume lay down in the grass, chin on his paws, and went on watching.

I stared up at the sky.

"I told you," said the ocelot, quietly, from somewhere beside my ear, "that you would be wise not to underestimate what you were carrying."

"You didn't tell me anything," I said, to the sky. "You've never told me anything. You keep *saying* things like that and not *telling* me things."

"Yes," the ocelot agreed. "I know. I am working on it."

I turned my head.

It was sitting in the grass beside me, its blue eyes steady and its coat bright in the sun, facing out across the field toward the long gray shape at the edge of it. They weren't looking at each other. They didn't need to.

"He recognized you," I said. "The wolf."

"He did."

"You know him."

A pause.

"I knew him," said the ocelot, "when he was a pup."

I opened my mouth.

"No," it said, very gently. "Not today, Eli. Please."

I closed it again.

Back on the walk to the village, that first morning, I'd told myself I was building a collection of things I didn't ask about. It had turned into a collection of things I *did* ask about and didn't get answered. Emilia's chest. Drayfus's face. The husk. And now a cat that had known a wolf when he was a pup.

The collection was getting big enough to need its own shelf.

Above me, the sky was a deep, cloudless blue. A thin line of smoke rose from the chief's chimney. Somewhere in the village, a small child was shrieking with laughter, and I heard Mara's voice, close by, calling out something about breakfast.

I lay there with my hand open on the warm grass, and I thought about a wolf who had waited on a shore for years, for a man who couldn't come back.

*I'm here,* I thought. *I'm sorry it took so long.*

It was exactly what Grandpa would've said.

I closed my eyes, and let the sun have my face.
