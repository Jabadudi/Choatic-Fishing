# Chapter 9 — Mara's Rule

Mara was the one who moved first.

She stepped past me, very slowly, took a long stick from the reeds at the water's edge, and used it to nudge the dark thing off the rock and into the folds of her cloak. She never touched it with her hands. When it was wrapped, she tied the cloak shut with two knots and held the bundle out in front of her like it might bite.

"Don't," she said, before I could say a word. "Don't say anything. You're going to say something funny, and I'm not in the mood."

I closed my mouth.

"We're taking it to Grandma."

"Mara, the ocelot said—"

"I don't know who that is, and I don't care." She started up the bank. "I know what it looks like. And I know what you look like when you hold the other one."

She didn't look back. I followed her.

Behind us, the river went on running. And somewhere low in my stomach, a very small voice said, *She is right. For once, I would like to be wrong about that.*

⸻

Emilia took it from her without a word.

We'd found her in the kitchen, peeling something at the long table, and she'd looked up when we came in and seen our faces and put the knife down. Mara set the bundle on the wood. She unknotted the cloak and folded it back.

The old woman looked at it for a long time.

She didn't touch it either. She didn't need to. I watched the color leave her face, slowly, from the forehead down, like water draining out of a basin.

"Where did you find this?" she asked.

"Eli caught it," Mara said. "In the river. Upstream of the docks."

"*Caught* it."

"On a line. On Grandpa's rod. It was just sitting on the bottom, Grandma, we didn't—" Mara stopped. She wasn't a person who stopped in the middle of sentences. "We didn't do anything to it."

"I know you didn't." Emilia sat down heavily, one hand flat on the table. "Child, I know."

"What is it?" I asked.

She didn't answer right away. She was still looking at the dull dark shape on the cloth, and there was something in her eyes I hadn't seen yet. Not fear. Not quite. It was older than that. A kind of grief that had stopped needing words a long time ago.

"It's a husk," she said finally.

"A husk."

"A Region Core that has been emptied." She spoke slowly, like each word was a step on a bad floor. "They don't die on their own. They can't. A core is part of the land, and the land doesn't die. But if someone were to take the power out of one, by force, and not by consent, what is left looks like this."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, someone was beating a rug.

"Someone *drained* it," Mara whispered.

"Someone drained it," said Emilia, "and then threw it into a river that runs through my village."

I remembered the ocelot's voice. *That should not be here.*

I remembered the way it had said my name.

"Whose is it?" I asked. "Which region?"

Emilia looked up at me. For a second, I thought she was going to tell me.

"I don't know," she said. "I have only ever seen one husk in my life. It was a very long time ago, and I was very young, and I promised myself I would never see another."

She wrapped the cloak back around it. Slowly, carefully, as if she were folding a flag. Then she stood up and crossed the kitchen to the far wall, where a squat iron-bound chest sat under a shelf of drying herbs. She took a key from a cord around her neck.

I had seen that chest before, I realized. The first morning. I'd noticed it and not thought about it.

She unlocked it. She set the bundle inside, on top of something I couldn't see. She locked it again.

"Nobody hears of this," she said. "Not Drayfus. Not the guards. Not the children, and not the neighbors, and not whoever's in your head, Eli."

My blood went cold.

"I don't—"

"I have lived a long time," said Emilia gently. "I know what it looks like when a man is listening to something nobody else can hear." She tucked the key back under her collar. "I won't ask. But you tell it that whatever it knows about husks, I would very much like to hear it. When it is ready."

I opened my mouth.

I closed it again.

In my stomach, the ocelot was perfectly, completely silent.

⸻

I didn't sleep well that night.

I lay on the straw mattress and stared at the ceiling and waited for the voice to say something. It didn't. Whatever the ocelot knew about husks, it was keeping to itself. The only sign that it was there at all was a faint warmth in my belly, like a coal banked for the night.

*Hey,* I thought, finally. *Are you going to tell me what that was?*

Nothing.

*You said it shouldn't be here. You said my name.*

A very long pause.

Then, so quietly that I almost didn't hear it:

*Sleep, Eli. I am thinking.*

I did not like how that sounded.

I lay there for a while longer. Outside, the village was quiet. Across the way, in the chief's house, I could see a line of lamplight under the door, burning late. I wondered what Emilia was doing in there. I wondered what was in the chest, under the husk.

I wondered, for the first time, how much of my own life other people were carrying around in locked boxes.

Then I slept.

⸻

"New rule," said Mara.

It was the next morning, and we were standing in the training circle, and the sun had been up for about fifteen minutes. I had run my eleven laps. I had eaten two bowls of porridge and a heel of bread. I was, by the standards of the last two weeks, practically a functioning human being.

Mara had not slept either. I could tell. She had shadows under her eyes, and she'd tied her hair back so tightly that her eyebrows were slightly raised. She had a practice spear in her hands, a long ash pole with a leather-wrapped tip, and she was holding it like a woman who wanted to hit something.

"Drayfus said you're allowed to use the rod now," she said. "With a witness. I'm the witness. So I get to make a rule."

"What's the rule?"

"No magic."

I blinked. "What?"

"Not until you can beat me once." She planted the end of the spear in the dirt. "One round. Without the rod, without the core, without whatever that glowing blue nonsense is that you do. Just you and a stick. If you can do that, you can cast all you want. If you can't, then you're going to cast and run out of power and fall on your face in front of a shadow walker, and I'll be the one who has to drag you home."

I looked at her.

"You're doing this because of the husk," I said.

She didn't answer right away.

"Grandma has never looked like that," she said. "In my entire life. I've seen her fight shadow walkers. I've seen her bury her friends. I've never seen her scared." She tightened her grip on the spear. "Someone is out there taking those things apart. And you have one in your *stomach.*"

"That's—"

"I know it's not your fault." Her voice cracked a little, and she cleared her throat and went on, louder. "But if somebody comes for it, you're not going to be able to cast your way out. Not at the speed you recharge. So you're going to learn to fight the old-fashioned way, and you're going to learn it from me, because I'm the one who's stuck with you."

I thought about telling her that I was scared too.

Instead I said, "Okay."

She looked surprised.

"Okay?"

"Okay. No magic. One round." I picked up the practice sword from the rack. It was shorter than the one Drayfus had used, with a wrapped hilt, and it felt a little less like holding a fence post. "What's a round?"

"Whoever lands the first clean hit."

"First clean hit. Got it."

"You're going to lose."

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"Almost definitely."

She stared at me for a second. Then she laughed, a short, startled laugh, like she'd been caught out, and the tension went out of her shoulders.

"You're so *weird,*" she said, and raised the spear.

⸻

I lost.

I lost the first time in about three seconds, because she was fast, and I hadn't expected how fast. The spear was a blur, and then it was tapping me gently on the sternum, and Mara was standing on the other side of it looking at me with polite concern.

"Again," she said.

I lost the second time. And the third. And the fourth.

By the tenth, I had stopped counting. It was a strange feeling, losing to Mara. Losing to Drayfus had been easy: he was a wall, a force of nature, a man who'd been killing monsters since before I was born, and there was no shame in it. But Mara was close to my age. She was shorter than me. She had a scratch on her cheekbone, scabbed over, and a mud stain on her knee, and she moved like water.

I couldn't stop watching her.

Not like *that.* Okay, a little like that. But mostly I was watching how she moved, and somewhere around the fifteenth loss, I realized that was the point.

*She doesn't telegraph,* I thought. *Drayfus watches your shoulders. Mara watches your eyes.*

I looked down at the dirt.

"Hm?" said Mara.

"Nothing."

She raised the spear. I didn't look at her face. I looked at the ground in front of her feet, at the small gray dust cloud that gathered when she shifted her weight, and she came in, and for the first time, I saw it coming.

I stepped back.

I didn't try to hit her. I just stepped back, one pace, light and balanced, and the spear tip whistled through the space where my chest had been.

Mara's eyebrows went up.

"Huh," she said.

She came again. I stepped aside. She turned and thrust and I dropped into the squat, weight in my heels, and the spear slid over my shoulder. It was ugly. It was nothing like Drayfus's lessons. But it was working.

> **[Still Water]** has activated.

*No,* I thought. *No magic. She said.*

> **[Still Water]** — *Passive. This is not a cast.*

*…Huh.*

"You okay?" Mara asked. She'd stopped. "You made a face."

"I'm fine."

"You're not even trying to hit me."

"I'm learning."

"You're *stalling.*"

"I'm *learning,*" I said, "how you move."

She went very still.

It was a small thing. Her eyes narrowed, and her weight shifted back onto her heels, and for the first time that morning, she looked at me the way Drayfus looked at his own students. Like she was measuring me against something and the number had changed.

"Then learn faster," she said.

She came in again.

⸻

The fortieth time, I won.

I want to describe it properly, because I think it's the proudest I've ever been of anything.

She thrust, high and fast, aiming for my shoulder. I'd seen it coming for half a second. I dropped, not away but *in,* under the spear, with my heels dug into the dirt and my knees bent exactly the way Drayfus had shown me. The wood sailed over my head. I felt it brush my hair.

And then I was inside her guard.

It was a stupid place to be. A spear is a long-range weapon. Up close, she was off balance, her arms overextended, the pole useless in her hands. She tried to pull it back. She didn't have time.

I touched my practice sword to the middle of her ribs.

Gently.

A tap.

Neither of us moved.

The sun had climbed. The light came flat and gold across the training circle, and a line of sweat ran down the side of her face, and I was crouched at her feet, breathing like a bellows, with the sword resting lightly against her side.

"…Clean hit," I said.

Mara looked down at me.

She didn't say anything for what felt like a really long time. And then something happened to her face that I'd never seen before. It crumpled, just for a second, and then it opened, wide and bright and unguarded, into the biggest grin I had ever seen on a human being.

"*Finally,*" she said.

She dropped the spear and grabbed my wrist and hauled me upright and held on to it, and for a moment, the two of us just stood there, grinning like idiots in the middle of the dirt, both of us out of breath.

"You *won,*" she said.

"I won."

"You *beat* me."

"I touched you with a stick."

"You touched me with a stick. That counts!" She let go of my wrist and punched me in the arm, which hurt, and then laughed, and then pressed the back of her hand against her mouth, as if she'd surprised herself. "I'm sorry. I'm just. I didn't think you'd do it today."

"Neither did I."

"I thought it would take until the end of the week."

"You're not very good at this whole encouragement thing."

"I'm *great* at encouragement." She was still smiling. She tried to stop and failed. "You're just very, very bad at fighting."

"That's a lot of compliments in one sentence."

"I know." She wiped her face with her sleeve, and I noticed that her ears had gone slightly pink. "I mean it. It was a good round."

*A good round,* I thought, and tried to keep my own face under control and didn't succeed.

I was aware that something was happening. I was aware, vaguely, that this was a nice moment in a long string of terrible ones, and that it was going to end, because it was a world where monsters came out of the forest at night and somebody was taking apart Region Cores and throwing them in rivers.

But I didn't want it to end yet.

So I did the only thing I could think of.

"Do I get my rod back?"

Mara laughed again.

"Yes," she said. "Go get your rod."

⸻

We sat on the fence afterward, the two of us, passing the waterskin back and forth. The circle was empty. Drayfus had come and gone, taken one look at Mara's face and my face, and said, "Good," in a tone that suggested he'd expected it a week earlier.

The sun was high. The air smelled of dust and cut grass and, faintly, of the kitchen.

"You know what's funny?" Mara said.

"What?"

"I was nine when I first beat Drayfus." She kicked her heels against the fence post. "He was letting me, of course. But I didn't know that. I was so proud I ran home and told Grandma, and she said, *good, now do it again when he isn't letting you.*"

"Did you?"

"Took me four years."

"That's a long time to wait for a compliment."

"That's what I said. She said, *a compliment isn't what makes you strong.*" She took a long pull from the waterskin and passed it over. "She's right. She's always right. It's very annoying."

I drank.

It was cool and slightly metallic, like it'd come from the well.

"Mara."

"Mm?"

"Back there, in the kitchen, your grandmother said something to me. About whoever's in my head."

The fence went very quiet.

Mara didn't move. She didn't say anything for a few seconds. Then, without looking at me, she said:

"I know."

"You… know."

"I'm not stupid, Eli." She was looking at her own hands, turning the waterskin strap between her fingers. "You talk to yourself. You do it all the time. You'll go quiet in the middle of a sentence, and your eyes go sort of… *somewhere.* Then you come back and say something that doesn't make any sense in context." She looked up. "Like this morning. You said *no magic, she said* out loud."

I hadn't known I'd said it out loud.

"That was—"

"You said *she,*" Mara said. "Not *you.* Not *I.* *She.*" Her eyes were steady and very serious. "I know I'm not the one it's talking to. I know you're not telling me. And I'm not going to ask."

"Mara—"

"I'm not going to ask. Because I'm not an idiot, and I know Grandma has been keeping secrets since before I was born, and I know that if you have one, it's probably the kind you're keeping for a good reason." She took a deep breath. "But if it's something dangerous, Eli, and you don't tell me, and you get hurt, I'll never forgive you."

The wind moved through the long grass at the edge of the circle. Somewhere on the roof of the chief's house, a Flitlynx made a sound like a very small kettle.

"It's not dangerous," I said slowly.

"Is it a person?"

"It's… not a person."

"Is it Oscar?"

I froze.

"No," I said. "It's not Grandpa."

"But it knew him."

I thought about the ocelot. The way it had said *Oscar* at the cabin, the way it talked about Drayfus, the long, careful silence in the kitchen.

"…Yeah," I said. "I think it did."

Mara breathed out.

It was a long breath, steady and slow, and some of the tightness went out of her shoulders. She looked at me for a while. She didn't look angry. She looked like someone who'd put down a very heavy thing and was still deciding whether to pick it up.

"Okay," she said. "That's enough for now."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Just promise." She held out her hand, palm up, and after a second I understood and set mine in it. Her fingers were callused and warm. "When you can tell me, you tell me."

"I promise."

"First."

"First," I said.

She nodded once. Then she let go of my hand, hopped down off the fence, and picked up her spear as if nothing had happened.

"Come on," she said. "I'm not done with you. You've won one round. I want ten more."

I laughed.

In my stomach, something very small and very smug, stirred, and settled, and said, in a voice that I could just barely hear:

*She is better at this than you are.*

*Shut up,* I thought.

*And she is right about the husk,* the ocelot added, quieter, almost to itself. *Which is the part I do not like at all.*

I didn't have time to ask what it meant.

Mara was already waiting in the middle of the circle with her spear raised, and her grin back in place, and she said, "Ready?"

I picked up my sword.

I was not.

But I went anyway.
