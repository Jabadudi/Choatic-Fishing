# Chapter 7 — Legs

Drayfus didn't knock.

He didn't need to. The door of the guest lodge opened on its own weight and a lantern came swinging in, and behind it a very large shape stood in the dark and said, in the same tone another man might use to tell you the house was on fire:

"Up."

I opened one eye.

The window was black. Not dawn-black, not even pre-dawn-black. The kind of black that has been awake all night and is not ready to hand anything over.

"It's not morning," I said.

"It is for you."

"The sun isn't *up.*"

"Then you have until it is." The lantern swung. "The perimeter of Willowback is eleven hundred paces. You will run it until the sun shows. If you stop, I will know."

"How will you know?"

"Because I'll be on the wall," said Drayfus, "watching your shoulders."

He left. He left the door open, which I took personally.

⸻

Here is a list of everything that hurt when I tried to sit up.

Everything.

The muscles in my thighs had gone past sore and out the other side into a kind of tight, buzzing numbness, like they'd been replaced with bundles of wire. My shoulders were stiff. My palms were raw where the bucket handles had been. And the hollow place behind my breastbone, the one the ocelot had called *reserves,* was still mostly hollow.

Mostly.

There was a little something in it now, like water at the bottom of a well. Not much. A cupful.

> **Reserves:** recovering.

"Good morning," said the ocelot.

It was on the stool again. I was starting to think the stool was its.

"Is this how the training goes?" I asked the ceiling. "Every day? Before the sun?"

"Eight weeks," it said.

"I'm going to die."

"No." It considered this. "But you will wish to, around the third week. That is normal. It passes."

I got up. It took a while. I put on my shoes sitting down.

The ocelot watched me tie the second lace.

"You did not dream," it said.

I stopped.

"No," I said. "I didn't."

"I thought not."

"Is that bad?"

It didn't answer right away. In the lantern light left by the open door, its blue eyes had gone narrow, the way a cat's do when something on the other side of a window has moved.

"It is interesting," said the ocelot.

I knew a lot of words now. *Interesting* was starting to sound like the worst one.

⸻

It was cold.

I hadn't expected that. In my head, a fantasy village was always some kind of permanent golden afternoon. But it was the hour before sunrise, and a damp wind was coming off the river, and the packed road under my shoes was hard as a plate. My breath came out in clouds.

I started jogging.

I would like to describe my form, but I think it's kindest not to.

The wall around Willowback was less a wall and more a long, uneven barrier of stacked stone and sharpened logs, with a walkway along the top. Every so often there was a guard on it, wrapped in a cloak, leaning on a spear. They watched me go by with the carefully blank faces of people who had been told not to laugh.

Somewhere around the first corner, I passed a guy asleep on his feet. He opened one eye as I went by, looked at me, closed it again, and said, "Morning, Oscar's boy."

Every guard said it. Every one.

*Morning, Oscar's boy.*

I started counting.

By the south gate, I had stopped being able to feel my feet.

By the docks, I was breathing like a broken bellows, and the wind off the water cut straight through my shirt.

By the east gardens, I couldn't have told you my own name.

And then, on the walkway above me, the sky behind the wall turned the faintest, palest gray, and I saw a shape standing against it. Tall. Arms folded. Not moving.

Watching my shoulders.

I put my head down and kept running.

⸻

The sun came up while I was bent over with my hands on my knees, trying very hard not to throw up on a flower bed.

It came up the way it does in stories. A thin line of gold at the edge of the world, then a bar, then a rush of warm light flooding over the roofs and the river and the fields, and every window in the village went bright at once.

It was kind of beautiful.

I hated it.

"Eleven laps," said Drayfus, from above me.

"I thought," I wheezed, "it was one."

"It was until you stopped counting."

I looked up. He was crouched on the wall with one boot on the edge, looking down at me. In the new light, the scar across his jaw looked paler than it had yesterday, and there was something almost like tiredness in his face. I realized he'd probably been awake the whole night.

"Water," he said, and tossed me a waterskin.

I drank half of it before I realized I'd stopped breathing to do it.

"You didn't stop," he said.

"I wanted to."

"That wasn't the question." He jumped down off the wall. He landed lightly for a man his size, and the gravel barely made a sound. "Mara has been running that wall since she was nine."

"Is that why she's so fast?"

"That is why she is still alive."

I glanced up at the rest of the village. The doors were beginning to open. Somewhere nearby a rooster started up, with the confidence of someone with absolutely no idea what was going on.

Mara came around the corner at a jog.

She wasn't out of breath. She wasn't even sweating much. Her hair was tied back tight, her sleeves were rolled up, and she moved with a long, easy, flat-footed stride, like running was just walking with ambitions. She slowed when she saw me, looked at the flower bed, looked at my face, and said, with real interest:

"Did you throw up?"

"No."

"You're about to."

"I'm *not.*"

"You're green." She stopped in front of me, hands on her hips, and I saw her bite the inside of her cheek. "Okay, fine, I'll be nice. You did better than I thought."

"How many did you do?"

"Thirty."

"Thirty *laps?*"

"I started before you," said Mara. "Obviously."

Obviously.

She looked at me for another second. Then she held out her hand, and I took it, and she hauled me upright like I weighed nothing.

"Come on," she said. "Breakfast. You'll need it."

I looked at Drayfus.

He had already gone.

⸻

Here's something about this world I hadn't expected: the food was *good.*

I don't mean good for a fantasy village. I mean the porridge was thick and nutty and had some kind of dried fruit in it that I didn't recognize, and there was fresh bread, still hot, and butter that tasted like actual grass, and smoked river fish cut thin and laid over the top. I ate three bowls. Mara watched me do it with growing alarm.

"You eat like you've been starved."

"I was running for two hours."

"I don't eat like that."

"You're built different," I said, around a mouthful of bread, and then heard what I'd said, and added, "I mean, like, you do it every day. It's, uh. You've adapted."

She gave me a long, level look, the kind you'd give a dog that had just very proudly brought you something dead.

"You're a mess," she said. But she pushed the rest of the bread toward me.

Emilia's kitchen was warm and small and cluttered with drying herbs hanging from the rafters. The old woman herself was nowhere to be seen. I'd wanted to ask her something. The day before, in the training circle, she'd said something about Grandpa that I hadn't been able to stop thinking about.

*Sometimes he'd laugh at it. Sometimes he'd argue with it.*

I hadn't told her about the ocelot.

I still wasn't going to. But I kept thinking about my grandfather, alone on the dock of the old fishing hut, arguing with something nobody else could see.

"What are you thinking about?" Mara asked.

"Nothing."

"You've been staring at that bread for a minute."

"It's very good bread."

She narrowed her eyes. Then she shrugged, which I took as a win.

⸻

Drayfus was waiting for me at the training circle with nothing in his hands.

No sword. No bucket. The rod was where he'd left it, leaning against the fence, and I looked at it for a while with real longing.

"Not yet," he said.

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were going to look at it until I let you touch it, and I'm not going to let you touch it." He turned his back on me and walked to the center of the circle. "Come here."

I went.

"Squat."

"What?"

"*Squat.* Like you're sitting in a chair."

I squatted.

He walked around me twice, slowly, the way you'd inspect a horse. Then he put one boot against my ankle and pushed it three inches wider.

"Back straight."

"It *is* straight."

"It isn't." He pushed my shoulder. "Chest up. Your weight is on your toes. Move it to your heels. There."

The moment he did, I felt it.

All the weight in the world came down through my thighs. My legs started shaking right away, a fine, fast tremble, like a plucked string, and my whole body wanted to give up. But it also felt oddly *solid.* Like I was standing on something, instead of just hanging above it.

"This is where your power is," Drayfus said, from somewhere behind me. "Not your arms. Not your head. Your legs. Everything you do will start here. If it doesn't, it's coming from somewhere it shouldn't."

I thought about the box.

*A man with weak legs bends from the waist and hauls it up with his back and his arms and his teeth.*

"Did you talk to the cat?" Drayfus asked.

I almost fell over.

"The—what?"

"The cat." He had come around the side of me, and he was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read at all. "Don't pretend. You stare at that corner of the stool when you think no one's looking."

"I don't—there's no—"

"Oscar did that too."

The squat held. Barely. My legs were screaming, but the world had gone completely still.

"The first week he was here," Drayfus said, "he used to sit on the wall at night. Talking to no one. I thought he'd gone soft in the head." He looked away, at the dummies by the fence. "I was a boy. I was an idiot."

"What did it say?" I asked. "To him. The thing he was talking to."

"I never asked."

"Why not?"

"Because I was a boy," said Drayfus, "and he was the strongest man I'd ever met, and I thought if I asked, he'd stop."

He didn't say anything else for a moment.

I stayed in the squat. It seemed like the only thing I could do.

Then, because I'd been waiting since last night and I couldn't hold it any longer, I asked.

"Did Grandpa ever talk about my grandmother?"

I was watching his face. The ocelot had told me to.

Drayfus didn't move.

Not at all. Not his hands, not his shoulders, not even his eyes. For about two heartbeats, it was like somebody had reached into him and pressed pause.

And then something behind his face closed, hard, like a shutter slamming down in a storm.

"Hold that," he said, "for another minute."

"Drayfus."

"A minute."

"Did he?"

"*Run,*" said Drayfus. "When you can stand, run. Eleven laps. Now."

⸻

I ran.

My legs gave out on the fifth lap. I walked the sixth. I did the seventh on my hands and knees, to see if it counted, and it didn't.

But I finished all eleven.

It was well past noon when I fell onto the grass behind the guest lodge and lay there, staring at a sky the color of a robin's egg, listening to the world go on without me.

Mara found me. She sat down next to me without a word and set something on my chest.

A flat, round loaf of bread, still warm.

"Don't tell anyone," she said.

I ate it in four bites.

"He didn't answer you," she said, after a while. "Did he?"

I looked at her.

"You were in the training circle," she said. "I could see from the fence. I couldn't hear. But I saw his face."

"What did you see?"

She pulled a long stem of grass out of the ground and turned it between her fingers.

"I've known Drayfus my whole life," she said. "I've seen him get stabbed. I've seen him bury a Rimejaw he raised from a pup. I've never seen him look like that." She was quiet. "Whatever you asked, don't ask him again."

"Why?"

"Because it's not that he doesn't want to tell you." Mara threw the stem away. "It's that he's afraid of what happens when he does."

I lay there.

In my stomach, in the place where the core had settled, something stirred. Faint and restless, like it was listening.

*Grandmother,* I thought.

I had never met her. Grandpa had never spoken of her. In the photograph, there'd only been one woman, with long dark hair, laughing at something off to the side. And I'd been so sure it was her.

I turned that photograph over in my mind.

*Long dark hair.*

Emilia, laughing. A much younger woman looking out of an old face, for just a second, when she'd told me her name.

It hadn't been my grandmother in the photo at all.

"Mara," I said slowly. "In the picture I found, there was a woman with Grandpa. A young woman. I think it was your grandmother."

She blinked.

"Grandma?"

"Yeah."

"That's not strange." Mara sat up and brushed grass off her knees. "She and Oscar were close. Everyone knows that. She was the one who found him, when he first came to this world. She taught him how to live here." She hesitated. "She loved him. A lot. But he didn't—"

She stopped.

"He didn't what?"

"It's not mine to say." Mara got to her feet. She looked suddenly very tired, and very young, and not at all like the girl who'd been laughing at me from a fence. "Ask her. Or don't. But not today."

She went inside.

I stayed where I was.

Overhead, a thin line of cloud slid across the sky, and the Flitlynx on the roof of the chief's house stretched its wings and went still, looking east, toward a horizon I couldn't see.

*Not my grandmother,* I thought.

*Then who?*

> **[Still Water]** has activated.

I breathed in. I let it sink.

But for the first time since I'd arrived in this world, it didn't feel like calm.

It felt like holding my breath.
