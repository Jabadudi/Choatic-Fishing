# Chapter 1 — The Old Fishing Hut

Being twenty-seven was supposed to feel different.

When I was a kid, I figured that by this age I'd have everything mostly figured out.

A good job.

Some money.

Maybe a girlfriend.

Maybe even my own place.

Instead, making money felt impossible, dating felt even worse, and somewhere along the way I had gotten very good at shutting myself inside and pretending that video games and anime counted as a social life.

Then Grandpa died.

He raised me and my little sister, Shylee, after our parents died in a car crash. I was twelve. She was three. He showed up at the hospital that night in his fishing jacket, still smelling like the lake, and he never really left.

Now it was just the two of us.

For a while after the funeral, I barely went outside at all. Shylee went back to school and her friends and her life, because she was eighteen and tougher than me. I stayed in my room and let the days blur together.

But Grandpa left me something.

Not money.

Not a house.

Not some secret fortune that would conveniently solve all of my problems.

He left me an old map.

It was folded inside an envelope with my name written on it in his shaky handwriting. There was only one location marked. A tiny island far off the coast.

And next to it, two words:

*Fishing Hut.*

On the back, in the same shaky pen, there was one more line.

*You deserve more than four walls, kiddo. Go live a little. It changed my life. Maybe it'll change yours.*

I read it until the words stopped looking like words.

*It* changed his life?

What was *it*? The island? Fishing?

The envelope was empty except for the map. I checked it four times.

I remembered the hut.

Or at least, I thought I did.

When I was little, Grandpa used to take me fishing all the time. Those were probably some of the happiest memories I had with him. Sitting beside him. Holding a rod that was way too big for me. Listening to him tell stories while we waited for something to bite.

His stories were the best part.

A lake with a stone road running along the bottom of it, so clear you could see the paving stones from the boat. Wolves that walked the shoreline at night and kept the fishermen safe. A girl who could whistle and make the fish jump into your basket.

He told them a hundred times, and the details never changed. Not once. Even when I tried to catch him out.

I figured he'd just told them so often he had them memorized.

He only took me to the island once. I was six, maybe seven. Shylee wasn't even born yet. I remember he didn't fish much that trip. Mostly, he sat on the dock and watched the trees, like he was waiting for somebody who was late.

When I asked who, he laughed and ruffled my hair and said, "Nobody, kiddo. Just an old man getting older."

After that, he went out there alone every summer until his knees gave up. He never invited me again.

I never asked why.

Now that I thought about it, I'd never asked him much of anything. He talked, and I listened, and I figured there'd always be time for the rest later.

Maybe that was why he left me the map.

Maybe he knew I'd need somewhere to go.

I stared at it for a long time.

"…Screw it."

⸻

Shylee was on the couch when I came out of my room with a backpack over one shoulder.

She looked at the backpack. She looked at me. She took one earbud out, which for her was the equivalent of a standing ovation.

"Are you… leaving the house?"

"I'm stepping out."

"Stepping out *where*? You haven't stepped out since the funeral. The delivery guy thinks you're dead."

I held up the map. "Grandpa's fishing hut. The island. Just for a couple of days."

She went quiet at that. She sat up, took the map, and looked at his handwriting on the back for a long time. I watched her read *you deserve more than four walls, kiddo* and press her lips together the way she used to when she was little and didn't want anyone to see her cry.

"He never took me there," she said.

"You weren't born yet. I was like six."

"Still rude." She folded the map carefully along its old creases and handed it back. Then she looked up at me, and for a second she looked a lot older than eighteen. "Go. He'd want you to."

"You'll be okay?"

"Eli. I've been feeding *you* for two months." She put her earbud back in. "Text me when you get there. And bring back a fish or something. A big one."

"Deal."

I was almost at the door when she said, without looking up, "Hey. Love you, dummy."

"Love you too."

I didn't think anything of it.

You never do.

⸻

Finding someone willing to take me there wasn't easy.

Apparently, "old fishing hut on an island from a hand-drawn map" wasn't enough information for most boat captains.

Eventually, I found an old man at the port who was mending a net on an upturned crate.

He glanced at the map. Then he looked up at me, and kept looking. Not the way strangers look at you. The way you look at a house you used to live in.

"…Eli?"

I hadn't told him my name.

"Is that you?" A grin cracked his whole weathered face open. "My, how you've grown. Last time I saw you, you were about knee-high and sick over the side of my boat the whole way out. How's your old man doing?"

It took me a second to understand he meant Grandpa.

It took me another second to say it.

"He passed. A couple of months ago."

The grin went away slowly, like the tide going out.

"Ah," he said. He looked down at the net in his hands for a while. "Ah, hell. I'm sorry, son. He was a good one. Best fisherman I ever knew, and I've known plenty."

He folded the map along its old creases, carefully, and handed it back.

"He told me you might come someday."

"He did?"

"Said if you ever showed up, I was to take you out and not charge you an arm and a leg for it." He snorted. "His exact words were 'the kid's broke.'"

Even dead, Grandpa was roasting me.

The price he named was almost a third of what everyone else wanted.

That should probably have been my first warning.

His boat looked like it had survived several wars and lost most of them. Technically, I think it was a dinghy. Calling it a dinghy was generous.

But the old guy promised it floated, and the price was the price, so I climbed aboard.

The trip took two hours.

I spent approximately a hundred and nineteen minutes of it regretting every decision I had ever made.

By the time we reached the island, I had discovered several exciting new forms of seasickness.

The old man laughed while I stumbled onto the dock.

"You'll get your sea legs."

"I'm never getting on a boat again."

He laughed harder.

Then he left.

I watched the little boat shrink into the distance, and only then did I realize I hadn't asked when he was coming back.

"…That's a problem for future me."

⸻

Future me showed up about five minutes later, halfway up the path from the dock.

I should probably call someone to schedule a pickup, I thought. The captain had written his number on the back of my hand in marker, which, looking back, was not the most professional system.

Also, I'd promised Shylee a text. If I didn't send one soon, she'd assume I'd drowned and start planning what to do with my room.

I pulled out my phone.

No service.

Not one bar. Not even the little "SOS only" that phones give you to make you feel slightly less abandoned.

I held it up over my head. I walked to the end of the dock and held it up there. I stood on a rock and held it up there, which accomplished nothing except nearly sending me into the water.

Nothing.

"…Great."

Well. If I was stuck here, I might as well see what I was stuck with.

I shoved the phone back in my pocket, walked up to the hut, and pushed open the door.

In hindsight, calling it a hut was ridiculous.

It was a proper wooden cabin sitting just beyond the shoreline, tucked into tall grass and thick trees. A little dock. A fireplace. A bed, a table, even a tiny kitchen.

Grandpa hadn't been out here in years. His knees wouldn't have let him.

So the dust should have been an inch thick.

It wasn't.

The floor was swept. The windows were clean. The rods on the wall had fresh line on them.

That was strange.

I decided, with the confidence of a man who had been seasick ten minutes earlier, that it wasn't my problem yet.

The shelves held what you'd expect. Nets, hooks, tools, a battered tackle box. And one small wooden lure box that was leaking light.

A faint blue glow, seeping through the cracks in the lid.

"…What the hell?"

There was only one thing inside.

A lure. At least, I assumed it was a lure. I'd been fishing plenty of times growing up, and I'd never seen anything like it. The soft kind, like a rubber worm, except it wasn't rubber. Dark and translucent, like deep-blue amber that somehow bent. It was heavier than it looked, and the surface was worn and scratched like it had been handled for centuries. Strange markings ran along the length of it. They weren't carved on it. They were *inside* it, suspended like insects in amber, and they were glowing.

Not brightly.

Just enough that I knew I wasn't imagining it.

I held it between my fingers.

For a second, I thought I felt something.

Almost like a heartbeat.

Then it stopped.

"…Nope."

I put it back in the box and put the box back on the shelf.

Grandpa could keep his haunted fishing equipment.

Even if he was dead.

*Go live a little.*

I was pretty sure this wasn't what he meant.

That was when I saw the photograph.

It was tucked into the frame of the little mirror by the door, curled at the corners and faded almost to brown. A young man stood on this exact dock, grinning like an idiot, holding up a fish nearly as long as his arm.

It took me a second to recognize him.

Grandpa. Maybe twenty. Younger than I was now.

There was a woman beside him, laughing at something off-camera, with long dark hair blowing across her face. And sitting at their feet, so big I first mistook it for a rock, was a gray dog.

No. Not a dog.

Its head came up to her hip.

I turned the photo over. Someone had written on the back, but not in any language I knew. Just a row of small, careful markings.

The same markings that were floating inside the lure.

I'd never met my grandmother. Grandpa never talked about her, and the one time I asked, he went quiet for so long that I never asked again.

I didn't know if this was her.

I realized I didn't know a lot of things.

⸻

The rest of the day was actually nice.

For the first time in months, I wasn't thinking about money. Or work. Or dating. Or what I was supposed to be doing with my life.

I just fished.

The water was calm. The air smelled like pine and salt. And for a little while, sitting on that dock with Grandpa's old rod in my hands, I felt like a kid again.

By evening, I had caught enough for dinner.

Cooking was one of the few things I was genuinely good at. I cleaned the fish, raided the cabin's suspiciously well-stocked spice rack, and cooked it on the little stove until the skin crackled and the inside stayed tender.

It was incredible.

Not "good considering I was stranded on an island" incredible.

Actually incredible.

Grandpa would've loved this. He'd have complained that I used too much seasoning, purely so he wouldn't have to admit it was better than anything he ever made.

I could almost hear him doing it.

That thought hurt a little.

But not in a bad way.

I propped the photograph against the salt shaker across the table, so it felt a little less like eating alone.

"So," I said to it. "Who is she?"

The young man in the photo kept grinning.

"Why'd you stop bringing me out here?"

Nothing.

"What changed your life out here?"

It was a stupid thing to do, talking to a picture. But I'd spent twenty-seven years letting him do all the talking, and it turned out I had a lot of questions saved up.

He'd just run out of time to answer them.

I fell asleep faster than I had in weeks.

⸻

The storm woke me up.

The cabin was dark except for flashes of light through the windows. Rain hammered the roof. Wind leaned on the walls hard enough to make the wood creak.

I reached for my phone.

12:30 A.M.

"Seriously?"

Thunder rolled somewhere in the distance. The storm wasn't violent yet, but it was getting there. Every few seconds, the trees outside bent beneath another heavy gust.

Then I remembered something.

I hadn't checked the weather.

Not once.

Before taking a tiny boat to an isolated island.

I closed my eyes.

"You're an idiot."

Grandpa would've never let me hear the end of that one.

The thunder grew louder. Closer. The gaps between the lightning got shorter, and the rain built until it sounded like a thousand fingers drumming on the roof.

I got up and went to the window.

Nothing out there but darkness and water.

I was turning back toward bed when I noticed the shelf.

Blue light.

The lure box was glowing again.

Not the faint little leak from earlier. Light was pouring through every crack in the lid, bright enough to throw thin blue lines across the far wall.

And it was pulsing.

Bright.

Dim.

Bright.

Dim.

Slow and steady, like something breathing.

"No," I told it. "We talked about this."

I didn't move. Neither did the light. It just kept pulsing, and after a moment I realized the rhythm felt familiar.

Bright.

Thud.

Dim.

Thud.

It was matching my heartbeat.

As soon as I noticed, my heart sped up.

So did the light.

"Okay," I said, to nobody. "Okay. That's not normal. That's extremely not normal."

I'd watched enough horror movies to know how this worked. The glowing cursed object starts doing something new in the middle of the night during a storm? You do not touch it.

That is how you die.

It could glow all it wanted. It could have the shelf. It could have the whole cabin. I was perfectly happy to stand right here and not look at it until morning.

Then I heard her.

A woman's voice.

Young.

Close.

Almost like she was standing beside me.

"Take it."

I spun around.

The cabin was empty.

"…What?"

The light on the shelf flared so bright I had to squint.

"Take it," the voice said again, urgent now. "You have to live."

I don't know why I listened.

Maybe it was the way she said it, like she already knew what was about to happen. Maybe I was just too scared to argue with a voice that wasn't there.

I crossed the room, flipped open the box, and grabbed the lure.

It was warm.

The storm exploded.

CRACK.

Lightning struck the cabin.

Wood burst above me. Part of the roof shattered open, and something slammed into my shoulder and threw me to the floor.

For several seconds, I couldn't hear anything except a sharp ringing in my ears.

My fist was still closed around the lure.

Then—

Howling.

A wolf.

Somewhere outside.

My stomach dropped.

"…You've got to be kidding me."

I forced myself onto my knees and looked through the gap the lightning had torn in the wall.

The next flash lit up the clearing.

Someone was standing outside.

A man.

Or at least…

I thought it was a man.

He was tall. His face was hidden completely in shadow.

And in his hands—

an axe.

He started walking toward the cabin.

"Nope."

I didn't need any additional information.

I shoved the lure in my pocket and ran.

I threw open the door and stepped into the storm. The rain blinded me instantly.

I made it maybe three steps.

Then something hit me.

Not physically.

It felt like electricity tore through my entire body, and it started at my pocket. The lure burned against my leg like a coal.

My muscles locked.

My legs gave out.

I crashed into the mud.

I couldn't move. Couldn't breathe properly. Couldn't even scream.

Through the rain, I watched the man with the axe come closer.

One slow step at a time.

I tried to crawl. My arms barely responded.

Ten feet.

Eight.

Six.

Then something shot out of the darkness.

A wolf.

Gray. Huge. Its head would have come up to my hip.

It slammed into the man hard enough to knock him off his feet, and they vanished into the mud together.

Snarling.

A shout.

The axe hit the ground.

That was the last thing I remembered.

Everything went black.

⸻

Warmth.

That was the first thing I noticed.

Then the smell of smoke.

And fish.

My eyes slowly opened. Everything was blurry. I blinked a few times.

There was a campfire nearby, and someone was sitting beside it.

A girl.

Around my age, maybe. Long dark hair. Pretty.

Really pretty, actually.

She was calmly tending the fire with one hand. In the other, she held a fishing rod, with a fish dangling from the line over the flames.

It took me a second to recognize the rod.

It was Grandpa's.

Something warm was pressed against my thigh. I didn't have to check to know it was the lure, still in my pocket, still warm.

I stared at her.

She noticed I was awake.

Our eyes met.

She smiled.

"You're finally awake."
