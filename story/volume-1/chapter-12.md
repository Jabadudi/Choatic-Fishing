# Chapter 12 — War Footing

I ran my laps.

I want that noted, because I didn't want to. I wanted to go back to bed and pull the wool blanket over my head and stay there until somebody came and told me it had all been a very detailed fever dream brought on by eating a glowing fishing lure. Which, honestly, was a pretty reasonable medical theory.

Nobody came. So I ran.

Ten laps of the path around Willowback, past the back fences and the kitchen gardens and the guards on their posts. Except this morning the guards weren't leaning on their spears. They were standing up straight, and they were looking east, and not one of them was trying not to laugh at me.

That was somehow worse.

Drayfus wasn't up in the watchtower. For the first time in a month, nobody was watching my shoulders.

I watched them myself. Back. Down. Breathe.

It didn't help as much as I'd hoped.

⸻

By the time I came around for the last lap, the square was full.

I'd never seen the whole village in one place before. I hadn't realized how *many* of them there were. A month of carrying water and running laps and cooking dinner, and I'd thought I knew Willowback. I'd thought, *a few hundred people, tops.*

It was a few hundred people, tops.

It's just that a few hundred people look a lot bigger when they're all standing in one place, and they're all quiet, and they all turn around at the same time to look at you.

I stopped at the edge of the square, sweating, breathing hard, wearing the shirt I'd slept in.

*Good first impression,* I thought. *Nailed it.*

The fisherman whose nets I'd helped mend was there, with his wife and three kids holding on to her skirt. The old man who sold me onions. The blacksmith, huge and soot-streaked, with his Rimejaw lying at his feet like a scaled, frosty rug, and the Flitlynx perched on its head. The girl who'd laughed so hard at my first squats she'd had to sit down. Women with babies. Old men with canes. Kids, everywhere, too many kids, sitting on their parents' shoulders to see.

All of them looking at me.

Then Emilia stepped up onto the rim of the well, and they all looked at her instead, and I started breathing again.

⸻

She didn't make a speech.

I'd been bracing for a speech. Something stirring. Something with a lot of *we shall* in it. I think, if I'm honest, I'd been half hoping for one, because a good speech would have given me something to feel other than what I was feeling.

Instead, she just told them.

Plainly. Quietly. The way she'd told me what the lure was, a month ago, by the fire. Shadow walkers were gathering at the old sluice ruins past Shellback Marsh. Thousands of them. Beasts among them. They were coming west, and Willowback was in their path, and they would arrive in twelve nights. Perhaps fourteen.

She didn't say how she knew.

She didn't have to. Somebody near the front turned around and looked at me, and then somebody else did, and then it rippled back through the crowd like wind through a wheat field, until I was standing in the middle of a few hundred people who were all very carefully looking at me and very carefully not saying anything.

I tried to stand up straight. Shoulders back. Down.

My shoulders weren't listening.

"The outsider."

It came from somewhere at the back. A man's voice, rough, not loud. Just loud enough.

"A month," the voice said. "A month he's been here. Thirty years we've held this village, and the first time we hear of an *army,* it's a month after he walks out of the woods with Oscar's lure in his pocket."

Nobody told him to be quiet.

That was the part that got me. Nobody agreed with him out loud, either. But nobody told him to be quiet.

I didn't blame them. I'd have been thinking it too.

I *was* thinking it too.

Emilia didn't look toward the voice. She looked at the whole crowd, slowly, from one side to the other, the way Drayfus looked at me in the training circle when I'd done something stupid and he was deciding how to explain it.

"Shadow walkers came to Willowback before Eli was born," she said. "They came to Willowback before *I* was born. They came four weeks ago and took a Rimejaw from us, and that was before anyone in this square had heard his name." Her voice didn't rise. It didn't have to. "They will come again after all of us are gone. That is what they are. That is what they do."

She let that sit.

"The boy did not bring them. The boy *saw* them. Because of what he saw, we have twelve nights instead of none." She turned her head, finally, toward the back of the crowd. "I would like to hear how you'd have spent those twelve nights, Hobb, if he hadn't."

There was a long silence.

Then a cough. Then a shuffle of feet.

Then nothing.

Emilia nodded, as if that settled something, and turned to Drayfus, who had been standing beside the well with his arms folded this whole time like a very large, very scarred statue.

"Tell them," she said.

And Drayfus told them.

⸻

Here's the thing about Drayfus that I hadn't fully understood until that morning.

I'd thought of him as a fighter. The big sword. The scars. The man who'd knocked me into the dirt ten times on my first morning and then made me carry forty buckets of water as a reward.

He was a fighter.

But standing on the edge of that well, with a stick in his hand, drawing in the dirt of the square while a few hundred people leaned in to see, he was something else too. He was a man who had been thinking about this exact morning for thirty years, and had been hoping the whole time that it would never come.

Willowback had no wall. I knew that. I'd run around the outside of it every morning for a month. It had back fences and kitchen gardens and the path, and the watchtower at the south end, and that was it.

"No wall," Drayfus said, as if he'd heard me thinking. "We can't build one in twelve days. We won't try. So we build what we can."

He drew the village. A rough circle. The lake along the bottom. The fields to the east. The forest curving around the north.

"They come from the east," he said. "Across the fields. They'll want the shortest line to the lodge, because that's where they saw the light." He didn't look at me. He didn't have to. "So we give them a short line. And then we make it hurt."

*Barricades,* he drew. Wagons, carts, timber, anything heavy, rolled across every lane on the eastern side, so the village became a funnel. *Ditches* along the field edge, knee-deep, lined with sharpened stakes, so anything coming fast came down hard. *Fire*: pits dug every twenty paces along the funnel, filled with dry brush and pitch, so that when the dark came, Willowback could make its own light.

"They hate fire," Drayfus said. "It won't kill them. It'll slow them, and it'll let us see them, and in the dark that's half the fight."

*The docks.* He drew a long line out into the lake.

"They won't cross open water," he said. "Every boat in Willowback gets cleaned, patched and tied up at the docks, with oars in it. Every family gets a boat or a place in one. When it starts, the children and the old go to the docks. If the line breaks, they go out on the water and they stay there until sunrise." He looked up, at the crowd. "If the line breaks, you do *not* come back for anything. Not a pot. Not a goat. Not a person. You go out on the water."

Nobody said anything.

"Everyone who can hold a spear holds a spear," said Drayfus. "Everyone who can't, digs. Everyone who can't dig, cooks for the ones who are digging." He finally looked at me. "Some people can do all three."

And that, as it turned out, was me.

⸻

I'm going to skip ahead a little, because the next twelve hours were mostly shovels.

I dug.

I'd thought the buckets were bad. The buckets were a spa day. The buckets were a gentle, relaxing walk with some water. Digging a ditch is like the buckets, except the ground fights back, and there's no trough at the end, and every time you think you're done, Drayfus walks past and says "deeper," without stopping.

By noon my hands had blisters on the blisters. By the middle of the afternoon, the blisters had given up and gone somewhere else, and I just had hands, raw and red and shaking.

But here's the thing.

Thirty-one days earlier, I couldn't have dug for an hour.

Thirty-one days earlier, I'd collapsed after twenty buckets and had to finish the rest on willpower and spite. Now I dug from sunrise to late afternoon, and my legs held, and my back held, and when I climbed out of the ditch to go cook for the work crews, I climbed out on my own. No hand from anyone. I just put my palms on the edge and pushed, and up I went.

Somewhere in the middle of the afternoon, a little blue box drifted up at the edge of my vision.

> **Strength — Rank increased.**

I looked at it for a while.

*Great,* I thought. *Love that for me. Can you dig?*

It didn't answer. The boxes never do. They just sit there, being smug, and then they fade.

⸻

I cooked for sixty people that night.

I don't know how. I really don't. There was a big iron pot the size of a bathtub hung over a fire in the square, and somebody kept bringing me things to put into it, and I kept putting them in. Fish. Onions. Some kind of root that tasted like a potato having an identity crisis. A whole basket of the little red peppers Mara swore weren't spicy, which were spicy.

The work crews came in from the ditches at sunset, filthy and silent and grim, and I ladled stew into bowls until my arm went numb.

Nobody called me *the outsider* while they were eating.

Nobody thanked me, either. Mostly they took their bowls and looked at me with a strange, careful, unreadable expression, and went to sit with their families.

I didn't know what to do with that expression. I didn't know if it meant *thanks* or *this is your fault* or *we know it's not your fault but we can't help feeling like it is.*

Probably all three.

Hobb, the voice from the back of the crowd, turned out to be a big, red-faced man with a bandaged arm. He came up last. He held out his bowl, and I filled it, and he looked at me for a long time without saying anything.

Then he went and sat with his kids.

There were four of them. The littlest one couldn't have been more than two. She fell asleep on his shoulder halfway through dinner, with a piece of fish still in her fist, and he didn't move for the rest of the night so he wouldn't wake her.

I watched them for a long time.

And sometime between the last bowl and the last of the fire, I made a decision.

⸻

It wasn't a hard decision. That's what scared me about it, later.

It was the easiest decision I'd ever made.

The army was coming for me. Not for Hobb. Not for his four kids, or the fisherman's three, or the girl who laughed at my squats, or the old man with the onions. It had looked up at the sky and seen *me,* burning like a lantern on the water, and it had said *I've found you.*

So the obvious answer was to not be found here.

I'd go east and north. Up along the river, toward the forest, toward the Listening Tree, toward anywhere that wasn't Willowback. I'd use Reedwater's Call, because the river was living water and I could cast more along it. I'd move by day and hide by night. And when the army came west, it would find an empty lodge and a cold trail. It would turn, and follow me, and leave the village alone.

That was the plan.

It was a good plan. It was simple. It made sense.

I packed while the lodge was asleep.

I didn't have much. A spare shirt. A water skin. A heel of bread and some dried fish wrapped in a cloth. The little knife Mara had given me, with thirty-one notches' worth of wear on its edge.

Grandpa's rod, last. I took it off the hooks on the wall and held it, and it was warm, and it hummed, very faintly, the way it always did when my hand closed around the grip.

"Where are we going?" asked the ocelot.

It was sitting on the windowsill. I hadn't heard it arrive. I never did.

"East," I said. "And north."

"Why?"

"You know why."

The ocelot looked at me with its blue, blue eyes. It didn't blink. It didn't argue.

"Yes," it said. "I suppose I do."

And it jumped down off the sill and followed me out the door.

⸻

I'd thought about leaving a note for Mara.

I'd sat on the edge of the bed with a scrap of paper and a stub of charcoal and thought about it for a long time. Then I'd put both of them down, because there wasn't a version of that note that didn't end with her coming after me, and the whole point was that nobody came after me.

So, no note.

It felt like a betrayal. It was a betrayal. I did it anyway.

I went out the back of the lodge and down the lane, past the barricade we'd started that afternoon, a half-built pile of carts and timber across the road with a gap left in the middle. Past the first fire pit. Past the start of the ditch, which looked a lot shallower in the dark than it had felt in the daytime.

*Deeper,* I thought, in Drayfus's voice, and nearly laughed.

Then I was out into the fields, and the village was behind me, and the grass was cold and wet against my legs, and the moon was a thin, dying sliver over the forest.

*Eleven nights,* I thought. *Maybe thirteen.*

*Plenty of time to be somewhere else.*

I'd made it about two hundred paces when something stepped out of the grass in front of me.

⸻

Brume.

He was standing in my path. Right in it. Not off to the side, not at the edge of the field where he usually sat and watched me run. Square in the middle of the track, with the moon behind him, so that he was a shape made of silver edges and darkness.

He wasn't growling. His hackles weren't up. He was just there.

"Hey," I said. "Hey, buddy. I need to get past."

Brume didn't move.

I took a step to the left. Brume took a step to the left.

I took a step to the right.

You can guess.

"Come on," I said. "Please. You of all— look, you *know* what's coming. You were standing there this morning looking right at it. You know it's coming for me. So let me *go.*"

Brume looked at me with those pale, ancient, unreadable eyes.

Then he looked past me.

"He's not going to let you," said a voice behind me. "I wouldn't bother."

I turned around.

Drayfus was sitting on the field fence about twenty paces back, one boot up on the rail, his sword across his knees. He looked like he'd been there for a while. He looked like he'd been there, specifically, waiting for me.

"How long have you been there?" I asked.

"Since sunset." He shrugged. "You looked at Hobb's daughter the way I looked at things, the first time I thought I could fix them by leaving." He nodded at the bag on my shoulder. "Bread, water, the knife, the rod. No note."

"How do you—"

"Because that's what I packed." He swung down off the fence. "I was nineteen. Got about as far as you did. Oscar was sitting on that same fence."

I didn't say anything.

There wasn't anything to say.

"Come on," said Drayfus. "She's waiting up."

⸻

She was.

The fire in the chief's house was lit, and there was tea in the clay pot, and Emilia was sitting at the low round table with her hands folded, exactly like she'd been the night before. As if she'd never moved. As if she'd been sitting there all night, waiting, knowing exactly what I was going to do and exactly how far I was going to get.

The ocelot went and sat on the hearthstones. Nobody but me saw it go. Brume stayed outside. I could see him through the window, sitting in the lane, facing the door.

I put the bag down. I didn't sit.

"I'm not wrong," I said, before anyone else could say anything. "You know I'm not wrong. It's coming for *me.* It said so. It looked right at me. If I'm not here—"

"Sit down, Eli."

"If I'm not *here,* it doesn't come *here.* It follows me. It goes *around.* Hobb gets to keep his kids, and the fisherman gets to keep his, and nobody has to go out on the water and—"

"Eli," said Emilia. "Sit. Down."

I sat down.

She poured me a cup of tea. She pushed it across the table. I didn't drink it. She didn't seem to mind.

"Tell me what you saw," she said. "In the dream. Before the marsh. Before the sluice. The very first thing."

"The village," I said. "From above."

"And?"

"And the lodge. It was glowing. Blue. Like a—"

I stopped.

"Like a lantern on the water," said Emilia softly.

She let that sit for a moment.

"The core flared, Eli. Here. In Willowback. Inside the training circle, with a Moonfang standing in it." She glanced, very briefly, at the hearthstones, where the ocelot was sitting. I don't think she could see it. I've never been sure. "That's what the voice saw. Not your face. It doesn't know your face. It saw a *light,* and it saw where the light was. And the light was *here.*"

"But if I take the light away—"

"Then they arrive at the fields twelve nights from now, and there is no light, and there is a village." She leaned forward. "Do you think they will politely turn around?"

I opened my mouth.

I closed it.

"Shadow walkers don't go around things," said Drayfus. He was leaning against the wall by the map, arms folded. "I've never once seen it. They go *through.* Whatever's in their way, they take. That's what the infected beasts are, boy. That's all they are. Things that were in the way."

He tapped the map. The sluice. Then the long curve around the marsh. Then Willowback.

Then he traced east and north, up the river, toward the forest. Toward where I'd been going.

His finger went through Willowback to get there.

"Look where we are," he said. "There's no road from that valley to anywhere you could run that doesn't go past this village first. You go north, they follow you north. *Through here.*"

I looked at the map for a long time.

"So I'm stuck," I said.

"No," said Emilia. "You are *here.* It isn't the same thing."

⸻

She was quiet for a while after that.

She drank her tea. She looked at the fire. Drayfus stood by the map and didn't say anything, and outside, in the lane, Brume sat in the dark and watched the door.

"I want to tell you something," Emilia said at last, "and I want you to hear the whole of it before you decide how to feel."

I nodded.

"If you leave," she said, "you will be alone in the forest with a month of training and a fishing rod. You'll move by day, yes. But you'll have to sleep. And the forest at night has its own shadow walkers, Eli, without any army to send them. You know this. You met one on your first night."

The cabin. The axe. The face full of nothing.

"You might last two nights," she said. "Three, if you're lucky. And then they'll find you, alone, in the dark, with the core inside you. And whatever has the core, after that…"

She didn't finish.

She didn't have to.

"You leaving doesn't make us safe," she said quietly. "It makes you dead, and them stronger. And then they still come here."

The fire popped.

I sat there, and I looked at my hands on the table. The raw, red, shaking hands that had dug a ditch all day. And I thought about Hobb's littlest daughter asleep on his shoulder with a piece of fish in her fist.

*It was the easiest decision I'd ever made,* I thought. *That should have told me something.*

"I wanted to do something," I said. My voice came out wrong. Thick. "I wanted to fix it. Everyone in that square was looking at me, and I thought, if I could just *go,* then at least…"

"I know," said Emilia.

"I don't know how to fix it."

"No," she agreed. "Neither do I." She reached across the table and put her hand on mine, the way Mara had the night before. Her fingers were cold and thin and very strong. "But I know you can't fix it from the forest. So stay. Dig. Cook. Train. Hold the line with us. That's how we fix it. All of us. Together. One night at a time."

I looked at her.

Then I looked at Drayfus.

Then I nodded.

"Okay," I said.

"Good." Emilia squeezed my hand and let it go. "Now drink your tea. It's been sitting there for ten minutes, and it's very rude to waste it."

I drank my tea.

It was cold. I drank all of it anyway.

⸻

Drayfus walked me back toward the lodge.

He didn't say anything most of the way. Brume padded along beside us in the dark, closer than he'd ever walked to me before, close enough that his fur brushed my hand. When we reached the lodge, he stopped at the edge of the lane, turned around, and sat down facing east.

He was still there in the morning.

"Drayfus," I said, at the door.

"Mm."

"When you were nineteen. When you got as far as the field and Oscar was sitting on the fence." I hesitated. "What did he say to you?"

Drayfus was quiet for a long time.

"He said, 'Not today.'" A corner of his mouth moved. It wasn't quite a smile. "Then he walked me home. Then he made me dig a ditch."

"…That's it?"

"That's it." He turned and started back down the lane. Over his shoulder, he said: "Ditch starts at sunrise. Don't be late."

*Not today.*

I stood in the doorway and listened to his boots fade into the dark.

I've heard a lot of people say those two words. Grandpa said them a hundred times. Grandpa said them about everything. *Can I have a soda? Not today. Can we go out on the boat? Not today. Can you tell me about the island, the real one, where you actually go?*

Not today.

And now I was finding him everywhere I looked. In a ditch. On a fence. In the mouth of a man with a sword across his back who'd been nineteen once and tried to run.

I went inside, and I put Grandpa's rod back on its hooks, and I unpacked the bag.

The ocelot watched me do it.

"You were going to let me come," it said.

"You'd have come anyway."

"Yes," it agreed. "But you were going to let me." It curled up on the stool and closed its eyes. "That was kind."

⸻

There's one more thing that happened that night, and I almost didn't put it in, because I don't really know what it means yet.

Emilia came and found me, after.

I'd just lain down. I heard a knock at the door, very soft, and when I opened it, she was standing in the hallway of the lodge with a lantern in one hand and a shawl around her shoulders. She looked smaller than she had at the table. More tired.

"Walk with me," she said.

We walked out to the end of the docks. The boats were there, tied up in rows, some of them already cleaned and patched, oars laid inside, ready. The lake was black and flat and still. The thin moon hung over it like a fingernail clipping.

Emilia stood at the very end of the dock and looked out over the water for a long time.

"Your grandfather used to stand here," she said. "Every night, for a summer. He'd fish. He never caught anything. I don't think he was trying."

I didn't say anything.

"I want a promise from you," she said. "Not a big one. A small one. The kind you can keep."

"Okay."

"If the line breaks…" She turned and looked at me. In the lantern light, her eyes were very bright. "If the line breaks, Eli, and Drayfus calls for the water, you take Mara and you go. North. Not to the boats. Away. You don't look back for anything. Not for a pot, not for a goat, not for a person."

"Not for you," I said.

It wasn't a question. She didn't treat it like one.

"Not for me," she agreed.

"Emilia—"

"Promise me."

I looked at her for a long time.

I thought about Grandpa on this dock, fishing for nothing, every night, for a summer.

I thought about the way she'd glanced at the iron-bound chest under the herb shelf when I'd said *you smell of her.*

I thought about how there were a lot of things on my *didn't get answered* shelf by now, and how a lot of them had Emilia's handwriting on them, and how I was starting to understand that she was going to keep some of them forever.

"I promise," I said.

She nodded. She turned back to the water.

"Why Willowback?" I asked. "The army. Drayfus says it's in the path. But it's more than that, isn't it?"

Emilia was quiet for a very long time.

"This village has sheltered things it should not have," she said finally. "People. Once. A long time ago." She pulled the shawl tighter around her shoulders. "Some debts don't get forgotten. They just wait."

"What people?"

"Another night," said Emilia.

And that was all I got.

⸻

The next eleven days went like this.

Run. Dig. Train. Cook. Sleep.

Run. Dig. Train. Cook. Sleep.

The ditch got deeper. The barricades got taller. The fire pits got filled. Every boat in Willowback got patched and tied up at the docks, with oars in it, and every family got a place in one, and the kids practiced running from their houses to the docks in the dark until they could do it without crying.

Drayfus changed what he taught me.

No more sparring for points. Now he put a shield on my arm, an old, heavy, round thing with a dent in the middle, and a short spear in my hand, and stood me in a line between Mara and a guard named Teo, and taught me to *hold.* Not to win. Just to hold. Feet planted. Legs bent. Shoulders back and down. The shield overlapping the shield beside it.

"You don't have to kill anything," Drayfus said, walking up and down the line. "You have to be a wall. For one breath. Then the next breath. Then the next. That's all a line is. A lot of people, being a wall, one breath at a time."

"I hate this," Mara muttered, beside me. Her shoulder was pressed against mine. Her shield was pressed against mine.

"You love this," I said.

"I *love* this," she agreed grimly, and set her feet.

I told Mara about the field, on the second day. About Brume and the fence and Drayfus and the tea.

I'd expected her to yell. She didn't. She went very quiet and very pale, and she didn't speak to me for the rest of the afternoon, and then, at dinner, she sat down next to me, put her bowl on her knees and said, without looking at me:

"If you ever do that again, I'm sending the Rimejaw after you."

"Fair," I said.

"And it won't be to bring you *back.*"

"Also fair."

She ate her stew. I ate mine.

After a while, she leaned her head against my shoulder, and she left it there for the rest of dinner, and neither of us said anything about it.

⸻

I filled in a scratch on the wall every morning.

One. Then two. Then five.

Seven. Nine.

Brume came every morning now. Not to the edge of the fields. To the lane outside the lodge, where he sat facing east, all day and all night, and only moved to walk beside me on my laps. Other wolves came too, now and then, gray shapes at the edge of the forest, watching. At night they howled, long and low, and the sound came from the east, and it was further away every night, and closer.

On the tenth morning, I filled in the tenth scratch.

On the eleventh night, the moon didn't rise at all.

Drayfus climbed the watchtower at sunset, and he stayed there, and he didn't come down.

I stood in the lane outside the lodge with the shield on my arm and Grandpa's rod across my back, and Brume beside me, and the ocelot on the fence post, and Mara on my other side, and I looked east, over the fields, at the dark.

"Tonight?" Mara asked quietly.

"Tonight," I said.

And somewhere out past the ditch, past the stakes, past the field edge and the long black line of the forest, very faint, the wolves stopped howling.
